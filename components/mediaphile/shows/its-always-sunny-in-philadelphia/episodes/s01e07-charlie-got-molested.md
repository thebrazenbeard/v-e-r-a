## Synopsis and beat map
News breaks that former school coach Chris Murray is accused of molesting former students. Charlie's abrupt reaction makes Dee and Dennis decide he must have been a victim; Mac responds perversely by becoming offended that he apparently was not selected. Charlie visits Ryan and Liam McPoyle and discovers that their allegations are fabricated revenge. The twins remind him that, while drunk in the past, he floated the scheme himself; they threaten to implicate him unless he supports their story. Dee and Dennis attempt amateur therapeutic intervention, while Mac visits Murray and effectively tries to discover why the coach did not find him desirable. The Gang tells Bonnie Kelly that Charlie was abused, producing a family intervention at which Charlie is pressured into a false admission. The McPoyles then take him to police to make the accusation official, but the scheme collapses under scrutiny.

## Character state
- **Charlie:** notably recognizes that false claims exploit real victims, but his own past drunken role in inspiring the scam gives the McPoyles leverage over him.
- **Mac:** turns abuse into a referendum on his attractiveness and strength, an extreme early example of narcissism swallowing the subject matter.
- **Dee / Dennis:** treat fragmentary psychology education as professional authority and make Charlie's situation worse through certainty.
- **McPoyles:** introduced as grotesque, conspiratorial opportunists with a preexisting childhood link to Mac and Charlie.
- **Bonnie Kelly:** debuts as emotionally volatile and intensely attached to Charlie.

## Themes / comic mechanism
The episode attacks self-serving certainty from several directions: false accusers exploit public concern; Dee and Dennis manufacture a diagnosis; Mac centers himself; and Charlie becomes trapped by a story everyone else wants to tell about him. The Gang's confidence repeatedly outruns evidence.

## Continuity / later relevance
- First appearance of Ryan and Liam McPoyle.
- First appearance of Bonnie Kelly and Uncle Jack in the family intervention context.
- Establishes the McPoyles as long-running adversarial acquaintances from childhood.
- Early strong example of Charlie being comparatively clear about one moral issue while still implicated by his own earlier recklessness.
