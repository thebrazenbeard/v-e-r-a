# Early Buddhism and Vedic/Brahmanical Development — Controlled Comparison V1

Status: COMPARATIVE_PACKET / REVIEW_REQUIRED

## Scope

This packet compares early Buddhism with the Vedic/Brahmanical layers reconstructed in PR #21. It rejects both 'Buddhism appeared from nowhere' and 'Buddhism is just reformulated Brahmanism.'

## 1. Karma and rebirth

Current diachronic control:
- mature karma/rebirth is absent from the Rigveda in the registered Jamison/Brereton reconstruction;
- early Upanishadic/Brahmanical sources witness major development of karma, rebirth, self, and liberation concepts;
- early Buddhist traditions presuppose karma/rebirth but reconstruct continuity without a permanent self.

Therefore the relevant comparison is not 'Vedic Hinduism always taught reincarnation and Buddhism borrowed it.' The historical problem is how several late ancient Indian movements developed competing models inside a shared and changing problem-space.

Current relation: SHARED_INTELLECTUAL_ENVIRONMENT / TRANSFORMATION; exact lineage unresolved.

## 2. Self

Upanishadic traditions develop major claims around atman and its relationship to ultimate reality. Early Buddhist aggregate analysis repeatedly refuses to identify changing constituents as self.

This is a substantive contrast, not merely different wording for the same doctrine.

Project inference: early Buddhist no-self teaching is especially informative when read against contemporary/near-contemporary self-and-liberation speculation, but V1 does not claim every anatta passage directly targets a specific Upanishad.

## 3. Sacrifice and ritual authority

Vedic/Brahmanical traditions elevate sacrifice, mantra, priestly expertise, and ritual correspondences. Early Buddhism develops a mendicant liberation path in which ethical conduct, mental cultivation, insight, and monastic discipline are central.

Buddhist texts can reinterpret Brahmanical status and ritual vocabulary, but rejection of one ritual-theological claim should not be generalized into rejection of all ritual. Buddhist ordination, recitation, offerings, relic practices, merit-making, and communal discipline themselves develop ritual forms.

## 4. Cosmic order and causality

Rigvedic rta links correct ritual/human activity with wider cosmic order. Buddhist dependent origination instead foregrounds conditional processes explaining suffering and rebirth without requiring a creator or permanent self.

These concepts are not equivalents.

Possible comparison: both traditions seek reliable order beneath apparent contingency, but the explanatory grammars differ sharply.

Current relation: HIGH-LEVEL_STRUCTURAL_PARALLEL only.

## 5. Gods

Vedic gods are central partners in sacrificial exchange. Early Buddhist worlds still contain devas, Brahma beings, Mara, heavens, and other superhuman beings, but awakening is not framed as dependence on a creator god's salvific action.

Therefore 'Buddhism has no gods' is false as a descriptive historical shorthand, while 'Buddhism is Vedic polytheism with philosophy added' is equally misleading.

## 6. Renunciation and liberation

Both early Upanishadic/Brahmanical and Buddhist materials participate in a wider ancient Indian turn toward liberation-oriented practice, knowledge, meditation, and renunciation.

McGovern's model locating Buddhism within a fluid Brahmanical/renunciant avant-garde is useful because it replaces a rigid two-religion opposition with a contact field. But the model remains contested scholarship and should not be promoted into direct descent.

## 7. Dependent origination and Vedic cosmogony

Modern scholarship has proposed that dependent-origination formulations may rework or parody Vedic cosmogonic language and structures. Such proposals are feature-specific hypotheses, not proof that Buddhist causality is simply borrowed Vedic creation myth.

V1 keeps these as future hostile-test candidates.

## 8. Strongest project inference

The Buddhist/Brahmanical case strengthens a project-wide rule:

Shared cultural vocabulary can coexist with deep semantic inversion.

Transmission analysis therefore needs to ask not only 'is the motif earlier elsewhere?' but also 'what operation did the receiving community perform on the motif?'

This is relevant to later On-Theo work on Judaism/Christianity, Christianity/rabbinic Judaism, and Qur'anic/Late Antique contact.
