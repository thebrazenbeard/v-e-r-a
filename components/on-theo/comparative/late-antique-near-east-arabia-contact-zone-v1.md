# Late Antique Near East / Arabia Contact Zone V1

Status: `COMPARATIVE RECONSTRUCTION / REVIEW REQUIRED`

## Purpose

This packet establishes the historical environment immediately preceding the Qur'anic/early-Islamic layer without making Islam the explanatory center of every preceding tradition.

The comparison includes:

- Byzantine/Chalcedonian Christianity;
- miaphysite Syriac/Egyptian Christianity;
- Church-of-the-East Christianity in the Sasanian sphere;
- Sasanian Zoroastrian and other Iranian religious institutions;
- rabbinic Jewish communities, especially in Babylonia;
- Arabian Jewish, Christian, Rahmanan-centered monotheist, and polytheist traditions;
- Aksumite and Red Sea religious/political networks.

## One geography, multiple overlapping networks

Religious traditions move through concrete carrier systems:

- merchants and caravan routes;
- soldiers and imperial garrisons;
- bishops, monks, missionaries, pilgrims and holy people;
- Jewish scholars and diaspora communities;
- royal courts and diplomatic embassies;
- refugees and exiles;
- scribes, translators and schools;
- captives, migrants and intermarriage;
- liturgy, poetry and oral teaching;
- inscriptions and monumental building.

A plausible transmission claim should identify at least one such carrier rather than relying only on similarity.

## Roman/Byzantine vs Sasanian is not Christian vs Zoroastrian

The empires are politically opposed, but their religious landscapes cross the imperial boundary.

Roman/Byzantine territories include religious minorities and Christians who reject imperial Christology.

Sasanian territories include large Christian and Jewish populations alongside Zoroastrian institutions and other religions.

Therefore:

`imperial border` != `religious border`.

## Christian plurality matters for later comparison

A Qur'anic or Arabian statement about Jesus, Mary, Trinity, crucifixion, monks, scripture, or divine sonship should not be compared against one generic `Christian doctrine` until the likely contact environment is bounded.

Candidate Christian interlocutor environments may differ in:

- Christology;
- language;
- biblical translation;
- apocryphal traditions;
- liturgy;
- saint/Mary devotion;
- ecclesiastical authority;
- relations with Jews;
- relations with imperial power.

A Syriac Christian parallel can be historically more plausible than a Latin one in some regions, but plausibility is not proof of direct dependence.

## Iranian contact without Iranian reductionism

Sasanian rule and Iranian religious institutions provide real channels into Mesopotamia and Yemen.

This makes Iranian concepts historically available in portions of the environment.

It does **not** justify explaining every dualistic, angelological, eschatological or judgment motif as Zoroastrian borrowing.

Required test:

1. establish source chronology;
2. establish semantic similarity beyond a generic motif;
3. identify a carrier/contact route;
4. test Jewish/Christian/Arabian alternatives;
5. preserve independent development as a live model.

## South Arabian monotheism complicates simple genealogy

Himyarite and later Aksumite/Abraha-period inscriptions show that names such as Rahmanan could function in different monotheist environments before Islam.

This creates several possibilities for Qur'anic `al-Rahman`:

- wider Semitic divine epithet continuity;
- South Arabian influence;
- Jewish/Christian biblical translation vocabulary;
- local Arabian theological reuse;
- combinations of these.

The project records the pre-Islamic distribution before evaluating lineage.

## Judaism in two important Late Antique zones

Jewish traditions relevant to the later Arabian environment cannot be reduced to one stream.

At minimum:

- rabbinic Babylonia develops under Sasanian conditions;
- South Arabian/Himyarite Judaism shows royal and epigraphic forms whose exact relationship to rabbinic institutions is unresolved;
- Arabian Jewish communities remembered in Islamic literature require independent historical verification where possible.

Thus `Jewish parallel` is not yet a provenance claim.

## Scripture and oral circulation

Texts can circulate indirectly.

A community does not need to possess a full codex of a source for a story or phrase to circulate through:

- lectionary/liturgy;
- homily;
- hymn;
- oral retelling;
- pilgrimage legend;
- polemic;
- translation;
- school tradition;
- hagiography;
- commercial/social contact.

Conversely, oral plausibility must not become an unfalsifiable excuse. The project should still seek earliest attestations and carriers.

## Religious vocabulary is highly translatable

Late Antique communities share or adapt terms for:

- God/Lord;
- mercy/merciful;
- spirit;
- word;
- messiah/anointed one;
- scripture/book;
- prophet;
- angel;
- demon/jinn-like spirit categories;
- paradise/hell;
- judgment/resurrection;
- prayer, fasting and almsgiving.

Shared vocabulary may conceal different propositions.

Semantic comparison must preserve:

`same word` != `same concept` != `same doctrine` != `direct borrowing`.

## Conflict and conversion

Najran, Roman-Sasanian wars, Aksumite intervention and Sasanian Yemen demonstrate that religion, empire and economic power can interact.

The project rejects both monocausal models:

- `religious conflict explains everything`;
- `religious language is only a disguise for material politics`.

The causal structure can be multi-layered.

## Chronological sequence for the next corpus phase

Before broad claims about Islamic origins, populate:

1. fourth-sixth-century South Arabian inscriptional religion;
2. fifth-seventh-century Syriac Christian source families;
3. Sasanian Church-of-the-East and Zoroastrian institutional evidence;
4. Arabian Jewish evidence by source class;
5. early Qur'anic textual system as its own primary corpus;
6. seventh-eighth-century Islamic tradition separately from Qur'anic text;
7. only then feature-level transmission hypotheses.

## Falsification tests

A proposed origin model should weaken if:

- the supposed source is later than the target;
- the crucial meaning is absent from the proposed source;
- the carrier route is anachronistic;
- a closer local antecedent exists;
- the resemblance occurs widely across unrelated traditions;
- the target polemic makes better sense as an internal development;
- the hypothesis requires combining mutually incompatible source communities into one imaginary interlocutor.
