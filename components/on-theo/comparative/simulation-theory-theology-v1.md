# Simulation Theory and Theology Comparative Matrix V1

Status: `COMPARATIVE RESEARCH / REVIEW REQUIRED`

## Purpose

Simulation theory and theology share structural vocabulary — creators, created worlds, transcendence, design, intervention, knowledge, purpose, world endings — but they are not the same theory.

This comparison is proposition-by-proposition and does not treat technological simulation as proof of religion or religion as ancient computer science.

## 1. Simulator versus classical creator God

A simulator in the ordinary technological sense could be:

- finite;
- embodied;
- temporally located;
- morally imperfect or indifferent;
- ignorant of some simulated states;
- dependent on hardware/resources;
- created by something else;
- part of a civilization;
- itself simulated.

Classical Jewish, Christian, and Islamic philosophical theology has often attributed radically stronger properties to God, including forms of:

- necessary existence;
- ultimate ontological independence;
- omniscience;
- omnipotence;
- perfect goodness;
- creation not dependent on pre-existing external hardware;
- ultimacy rather than membership in a larger causal world.

Therefore:

`SIMULATOR` != `CLASSICAL THEISTIC GOD`.

They can nevertheless share one structural relation:

`world W ontologically depends on an agent/process outside W`.

That relation alone is too weak to identify the two concepts.

## 2. Demiurge comparison

A finite simulator is structurally closer in some respects to **demiurgic** creator models than to classical creation ex nihilo.

A demiurge may organize a world while depending on realities it did not create.

Likewise, a programmer may organize simulated matter/laws while depending on:

- parent-universe physics;
- computational substrate;
- energy;
- mathematics;
- pre-existing hardware;
- social institutions.

This is a `THEOLOGICAL_COMPARISON`, not an assertion that ancient demiurge traditions described computers.

## 3. Creation ex nihilo

A conventional simulation begins from a substrate and resources.

Even if simulated inhabitants experience a world beginning from `nothing` within their physics, the implementation still depends on parent-level conditions.

Classical creation ex nihilo claims ultimate creation without pre-existing co-eternal matter.

Therefore a programmer launching a simulation is normally a poor literal analogue for creation ex nihilo.

## 4. Transcendence and immanence

A simulator can be `outside` simulated space while causally acting within it.

That yields a useful model of **relative transcendence**:

- simulator not spatially located inside simulated coordinates;
- simulator causally related to simulated events;
- simulator may instantiate avatars/interfaces inside the world.

But relative transcendence is not metaphysical ultimacy. The simulator may occupy ordinary space in a parent universe.

Project distinction:

`OUTSIDE SIMULATED SPACETIME` != `OUTSIDE ALL SPACE/TIME/CAUSAL DEPENDENCE`.

## 5. Omniscience and observability

A naive simulation analogy suggests the programmer can inspect every simulated state.

That need not follow.

Constraints can include:

- encryption;
- distributed computation;
- emergent complexity;
- computational irreducibility;
- undecidability;
- limited storage/logging;
- inaccessible internal semantic properties.

Wolpert's computer-science framing is especially useful here: `runs a simulation` does not automatically mean `can decide every nontrivial fact about it`.

Thus simulator knowledge should not be equated with theological omniscience.

## 6. Omnipotence and intervention

A simulator may have privileged intervention abilities:

- pause;
- alter state;
- change rules;
- restore checkpoints;
- create/delete agents;
- inject messages;
- fork timelines.

To simulated inhabitants, such acts could look miraculous.

But the simulator remains limited by parent-level rules/resources unless the model stipulates otherwise.

A technologically overwhelming agent is not thereby omnipotent in the theological sense.

## 7. Incarnation / avatar analogy

A simulator or operator could instantiate an in-world avatar that:

- has a simulated body;
- receives external control/input;
- communicates with inhabitants;
- possesses privileged information.

This creates a structural analogy with incarnation/avatar traditions.

But important differences remain:

- Christian incarnation involves specific claims about identity, divine/human natures, salvation, history, and embodiment;
- Hindu avatar traditions arise within different metaphysical and devotional systems;
- a game avatar is normally an instrument/interface, not automatically the same kind of identity claim.

Therefore `avatar` is a useful model for one relation but not an explanatory replacement for incarnation theology.

## 8. Revelation as communication across levels

A simulator could communicate information unavailable through ordinary in-world causal processes.

This provides a model of `cross-level revelation`.

It does not establish that any historical revelation claim has such an origin.

Authentication problems remain:

- in-world deception;
- competing external agents;
- false simulator claims;
- altered memories;
- ambiguous messages.

The simulation analogy therefore sharpens rather than solves revelation epistemology.

## 9. Miracles

In a simulation, an external intervention could violate ordinary in-world regularities while remaining lawful at the parent level.

This gives a coherent model in which:

`local law violation` + `higher-level lawful cause` can coexist.

But it does not show that historical miracle reports are simulator interventions.

The analogy mainly demonstrates that a law-governed world can, in principle, be causally open to a higher implementation level.

## 10. Eschatological termination

A simulation can end through:

- shutdown;
- resource exhaustion;
- intentional termination;
- reset;
- migration to another substrate;
- state preservation/restoration.

These operations resemble some formal aspects of eschatological ideas — world ending, transformation, judgment-like selection, restoration — but resemblance is weak unless a specific theological proposition is compared.

`server shutdown` is not a historical explanation of apocalypse literature.

## 11. Resurrection / restoration analogy

A saved information-state could hypothetically instantiate a person again after simulated death.

Questions immediately appear:

- Is restored copy numerically the same person?
- Does continuity require uninterrupted consciousness?
- Are multiple restored copies the same person?
- Is identity informational, bodily, causal, or soul-based?

Simulation therefore supplies a useful thought experiment for resurrection/personal-identity philosophy without proving any religious resurrection claim.

## 12. Soul and substrate

Simulation thought experiments make the soul/body problem more explicit.

Possible models:

1. simulated minds are entirely implemented by simulated/computational states;
2. conscious subjects exist at the parent level and receive simulated sensory input;
3. a non-computational consciousness couples to simulated agents;
4. no simulated entity is genuinely conscious.

Each maps differently onto historical dualist/materialist/soul traditions.

The simulation hypothesis itself does not choose among them.

## 13. Nested creators and regress

If simulators are themselves simulated, creator chains can be nested:

`world A creates B; agents in B create C; ...`

This resembles some nested-cosmos and emanation hierarchies structurally.

But it creates the opposite of theological ultimacy unless the chain terminates in a necessary/base reality.

Finding a local creator would therefore not answer `Why is there anything at all?`.

## 14. Theodicy

If suffering occurs in an intentionally designed simulation, questions about simulator responsibility resemble the problem of evil.

Possible simulator motives:

- scientific study;
- entertainment;
- education;
- historical reconstruction;
- optimization/training;
- indifference to individual suffering;
- malicious intent;
- emergent unintended consequences.

Unlike classical theism, a simulator need not be perfectly good or omnipotent. That can make suffering easier to explain under some simulation models, but at the cost of abandoning the traditional divine attributes.

Simulation therefore changes the theodicy problem rather than solving it universally.

## 15. Fine-tuning: simulation as alternative design hypothesis

### Moti Mizrahi, 2017

Source:
- `The Fine-Tuning Argument and the Simulation Hypothesis`, *Think* 16.46 (2017), 93-102.
- https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/think/article/abs/finetuning-argument-and-the-simulation-hypothesis/BA286B9FD7406DCF1AE5A95FDBC20CDC

Mizrahi argues that simulation provides an alternative explanation of apparent fine-tuning and therefore weakens an inference from fine-tuning specifically to a supernatural designer.

Project classification:
- `PHILOSOPHICAL_ARGUMENT / SCHOLARLY_INTERPRETATION`.

### Miles K. Donahue, 2026

Source:
- `Of Simulations and Conscious Universes: Explaining Fine-Tuning Without God`, *Canadian Journal of Philosophy*.
- https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/canadian-journal-of-philosophy/article/of-simulations-and-conscious-universes-explaining-finetuning-without-god/25F08274C9B7FB3859A13405B938D09C

Donahue compares theism with several alternatives, including simulation, teleological cosmopsychism, axiarchism, and teleological laws, using a Bayesian framework. Donahue ultimately argues that theism is superior to those alternatives under his analysis.

This is useful precisely because it reaches a different conclusion from Mizrahi.

Project guard:

On-Theo will preserve both arguments and their assumptions rather than manufacture a winner from the mere fact that both are Bayesian/philosophical comparisons.

## 16. Fine-tuning likelihood problem

To use simulation as an explanation of life-permitting constants, we need assumptions about simulator motives.

If simulators commonly create life-supporting worlds, fine-tuning may be expected.

If simulators mostly create sterile physics experiments, random worlds, abstract games, or worlds with different laws, that likelihood changes.

Thus:

`P(fine-tuning | simulation)` is not defined by `simulation` alone.

It depends on a simulator-goal distribution.

The same general problem applies to theological design arguments when divine motives are underspecified.

## 17. Historical analogues

### Plato's cave

Structural theme: experienced world versus a deeper causal reality.

Guard: the cave is an epistemic/allegorical model, not computer simulation.

### Dream skepticism

Structural theme: apparently coherent experience with a different generating reality.

Guard: dreaming is not external computational implementation.

### Demiurgic traditions

Structural theme: world-ordering agent distinct from ultimate reality.

Potentially strong structural comparator for finite simulators.

Guard: no technological/computational claim implied.

### Illusion/reality traditions

Some Indian philosophical/religious traditions discuss appearance, māyā, mistaken identification, or levels of reality.

Guard: `māyā = simulation` is anachronistic and semantically destructive. Each school/tradition must be reconstructed on its own terms before comparison.

### Emanation / layered cosmologies

Structural theme: multiple ontological levels.

Guard: hierarchy of being != nested computer virtualization.

## 19. Project inferences

### PI-1: Simulation is a useful anti-equivocation tool for design arguments

Simulation makes clear that `designer` does not entail `ultimate supernatural God`.

This is a philosophical clarification, not evidence that a simulator exists.

### PI-2: Simulation provides a model of relative transcendence

An agent can be outside a world's coordinate system while remaining finite and natural in a higher-level world.

Therefore `transcendent relative to our spacetime` does not entail `metaphysically ultimate`.

### PI-3: Simulation sharpens revelation/miracle authentication problems

Even direct cross-level intervention would not automatically identify the intervener as morally perfect, ultimate, or truthful.

### PI-4: Local creator discovery would not solve ultimate cosmology

A simulator explains the local world's implementation while pushing the existence question into the parent universe.

## 20. V1 limits

No V1 evidence establishes:

- that a historical religion originated from simulator contact;
- that ancient `illusion` traditions encoded computer science;
- that a simulator is God;
- that God is a simulator;
- that fine-tuning uniquely supports simulation;
- that simulation resolves the problem of evil;
- that resurrection is equivalent to restoring a saved computational state.

Those remain comparisons or speculative models unless independently supported.
