# World Traditions Expansion V1

Status: RESEARCH_INTAKE / ACTIVE

## Purpose

This expansion adds the traditions Patrick named on 2026-09-17 to the broad On-Theo corpus without flattening living, historical, revived, or polemically invented categories into one undifferentiated list of 'religions.'

Requested research families:
- Shinto
- Daoism / Taoism
- Hindu traditions beyond the already-mapped Vedic/Brahmanical/Upanishadic foundation
- ancient Norse religion and modern Ásatrú/Heathenry
- Lakota religious and ceremonial traditions
- Satanism
- 'devil worship' as both a self-description question and a historical accusation category
- Sumerian religion as a deeper layer inside the existing Mesopotamian corpus

## Method rule

Each family gets an internal diachronic reconstruction before cross-tradition comparison. Modern revival, outsider accusation, later sectarian synthesis, and ancient source religion remain distinct evidence layers.

## 1. Shinto

### Required slices
- early kami/court/local cult evidence;
- Kojiki and Nihon shoki mythic/ritual materials;
- shrine institutions and imperial ritual;
- long Buddhist-Shinto interaction and combinatory systems;
- medieval Shinto theologies;
- early modern kokugaku / nativist developments;
- Meiji restructuring, State Shinto, shrine administration and imperial ideology;
- post-1945 Shrine Shinto, sect Shinto, local/festival practice and contemporary institutions.

### Guard

Do not treat Shinto as a timeless, unchanged 'indigenous animism.' Modern Shinto scholarship explicitly challenges that transhistorical model. Kokugakuin's Encyclopedia of Shinto itself divides the field into shrine, sectarian, imperial, folk, and scholastic/philosophical forms.

### Initial scholarly/source anchors
- Kokugakuin University, Encyclopedia of Shinto, 'Introduction: The History of Shinto';
- Helen Hardacre, Shinto: A History;
- Mark Teeuwen and John Breen, histories of Shinto and Ise;
- primary mythic/ritual witnesses to be registered separately from modern histories.

## 2. Daoism / Taoism

### Required slices
- Warring States textual traditions: Daodejing, Zhuangzi and related currents;
- manuscript history, especially Guodian and Mawangdui textual evidence;
- Huang-Lao and early imperial developments;
- Celestial Masters;
- Shangqing;
- Lingbao;
- Daoist canon formation;
- ritual, talismans, exorcism, registers, liturgy and local cult interaction;
- alchemical and longevity traditions;
- Quanzhen and later institutional developments;
- modern/local Daoist communities.

### Guard

Do not enforce the old 'philosophical Daoism' versus 'religious Daoism' binary as if ancient and medieval Daoists used that Western distinction. Scholarship on the Daoist canon shows the categories are historically entangled.

### Initial scholarly/source anchors
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Daoism / Religious Daoism entries;
- Russell Kirkland;
- Kristofer Schipper and Franciscus Verellen;
- Livia Kohn and Fabrizio Pregadio reference works.

## 3. Hindu traditions beyond the Vedic foundation

The repo already distinguishes Rigvedic, later Vedic/Brahmanical and early Upanishadic developments. 'Hinduism' will therefore be expanded forward, not used retroactively as one timeless system.

### Required slices
- Mahabharata and Ramayana religious worlds;
- Bhagavad Gita and related synthesis;
- Puranic cosmologies and genealogies;
- Vaishnava traditions;
- Shaiva traditions;
- Shakta/Devi traditions;
- bhakti movements;
- temple and image worship;
- tantra across multiple traditions;
- Vedanta schools as historically distinct philosophical-theological developments;
- Smarta synthesis;
- regional, village and local traditions;
- modern reform and global Hindu movements.

### Guard

Vishnu, Shiva/Rudra, Devi, brahman, atman, karma, dharma, bhakti, moksha and avatara must each be period- and tradition-bound. Later classical meanings cannot be projected wholesale into the Rigveda.

## 4. Ancient Norse religion vs modern Ásatrú / Heathenry

### Ancient Norse/Germanic slice
- archaeological evidence;
- runic evidence where religiously relevant;
- skaldic and eddic poetry;
- Snorri's Prose Edda as a later Christian-era literary preservation layer;
- gods, landvættir and other beings;
- blót and cult;
- seiðr and ritual specialists;
- kingship and cult;
- death/afterlife plurality;
- fate, Ragnarök and cosmology;
- regional and chronological variation.

### Modern revival slice
- Icelandic Ásatrú revival;
- modern Heathenry/Ásatrú organizations;
- reconstruction methodology;
- differences among inclusive, folkish and other modern currents;
- modern ethics, identity and ritual innovation.

### Guard

Modern Ásatrú/Heathenry is reconstructed religion, not demonstrated uninterrupted survival of Viking-age religion. The Troth explicitly describes modern Heathenry as reconstruction and notes no evidence of continuous secret survival after medieval Christianization.

## 5. Lakota religious and ceremonial traditions

### Naming and sovereignty guard

Use 'Lakota' where the evidence concerns Lakota people. 'Sioux' is a broader/external historical label and must not silently homogenize Lakota, Dakota and Nakota traditions.

Prioritize Lakota/tribal voices, language, tribal institutions and Indigenous-centered scholarship. Ethnographic and government sources are useful but should not outrank living-community self-description when discussing current sacred practice.

### Research slices
- Wakan / Wakan Tanka terminology and semantic range;
- creation/emergence traditions;
- Black Hills / He Sapa sacred geography;
- Wind Cave/Oniya Ohloka emergence traditions;
- Bear Lodge / Mato Tipila traditions;
- pipe traditions;
- sweat lodge and purification;
- Sun Dance;
- vision-seeking/fasting traditions;
- sacred beings, thunder beings, buffalo relations and star knowledge;
- ceremonial specialists;
- kinship and community;
- effects of colonial suppression, missionization and legal restriction;
- contemporary revival/continuity and sacred-site protection.

### Guard

Do not publish restricted ceremonial detail merely because an outsider source recorded it. Treat sacred knowledge and public knowledge as different access classes.

## 6. Satanism

### Required historical distinction

'Satanism' has served both as an accusation against enemies and as a modern self-identified religious/philosophical category.

Research slices:
- medieval/early-modern accusations and imagined Satanism;
- witchcraft/demonological constructions;
- Black Mass legends and anti-heretical polemic;
- Romantic literary Satanism;
- nineteenth-century occult currents;
- Church of Satan / LaVeyan Satanism;
- Temple of Set as related but distinct Setian development;
- The Satanic Temple;
- explicitly theistic Satanisms;
- other modern groups, with extremist organizations treated separately rather than normalized into the whole category;
- Satanic Panic and modern conspiracy narratives.

Joseph Laycock's 2023 Cambridge overview is a key starting map because it follows the term from accusation and legend into modern self-identified Satanic religions.

## 7. 'Devil worship'

Treat this as a separate research problem rather than a synonym for Satanism.

Questions:
- Which groups, if any, actually self-identify as worshipping a literal Christian Satan/devil?
- When is 'devil worship' an outsider accusation rather than self-description?
- How were Jews, heretics, witches, Indigenous religions, new religious movements and political enemies historically accused of serving Satan?
- How did modern Satanic Panic narratives construct hidden devil-worship cults?
- How do literal/theistic Satanists distinguish themselves from atheistic or symbolic Satanists?

Default classification: ACCUSATION_CATEGORY unless a source demonstrates self-identification or practice.

## 8. Sumerian religion

The existing Mesopotamian packet remains broad. This new slice isolates Sumerian-language and city-state traditions before later Akkadian/Babylonian/Assyrian transformations.

### Required slices
- An, Enlil, Enki, Inana, Nanna, Utu and major city cults;
- divine assembly;
- me / divine-cultural powers and offices;
- temple institutions and economic-social role;
- kingship and divine legitimation;
- city patron gods and political theology;
- creation/order traditions;
- Enki and world-order traditions;
- Inana's descent and underworld traditions;
- Dumuzi cycles;
- death, ghosts and underworld;
- flood traditions;
- lamentation and city destruction;
- scribal transmission into later Mesopotamian religion.

### Guard

Sumerian religion != Babylonian religion. Later Akkadian-language compositions may preserve, transform, translate or replace Sumerian material and must be separately dated.

### Initial source anchor

The Oxford Electronic Text Corpus of Sumerian Literature provides transliterations/translations of Sumerian literary compositions including Enki and the World Order and Inana's Descent; individual manuscript history and critical editions still need separate witness controls.
