# Simulation Argument and Bayesian Models V1

## 1. Bostrom's argument is a disjunction

Nick Bostrom's 2003 paper does **not** conclude simply that we are probably simulated.

Its headline conclusion is that at least one of three propositions is true:

1. the fraction of human-level civilizations that reach a posthuman stage is extremely small;
2. the fraction of posthuman civilizations that are interested in running significant numbers of ancestor simulations is extremely small;
3. the fraction of observers with human-like experiences who are living in ancestor simulations is extremely close to one.

The argument's force comes from conditional population reasoning: if many posthuman civilizations exist, if they have the resources to run vast numbers of ancestor simulations, if they choose to do so, and if the simulated persons count as conscious observers of the relevant reference class, then simulated observers could vastly outnumber original observers.

Source:
- Nick Bostrom, `Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?`, *The Philosophical Quarterly* 53.211 (2003), 243-255.
- https://simulation-argument.com/simulation/

## 2. Simulation argument != simulation hypothesis

Bostrom's own later FAQ stresses that one may accept the argument while rejecting the simulation hypothesis by assigning high probability to the first or second disjunct.

This distinction is mandatory in On-Theo:

- `SIMULATION_ARGUMENT` = the conditional disjunction/population argument.
- `SIMULATION_HYPOTHESIS` = the proposition that we are actually in a simulation.

Conflating them converts a conditional argument into a stronger claim than its author makes.

## 3. Load-bearing assumption: substrate independence

Bostrom explicitly introduces substrate independence before the population argument.

His needed assumption is weaker than an unrestricted doctrine that every functionally equivalent computer must be conscious. The paper requires that a sufficiently detailed computational replication of the relevant human brain processes could instantiate subjective experience.

That still matters enormously.

If no digital/computational implementation can instantiate phenomenal consciousness, then an ancestor simulation may contain convincing behavioral agents without adding conscious observer-moments to the population count.

The argument would then need a different observer measure or would lose much of its intended force.

## 4. Posthuman computational capacity

Bostrom estimates enormous future computational capacity under assumptions about mature nanotechnology/planetary-scale computation and uses those estimates to motivate the feasibility of large numbers of ancestor simulations.

V1 guard:

`future computational capacity estimate` != `demonstrated feasibility of conscious ancestor simulation`.

The feasibility chain contains at least:

1. enough raw physical computation;
2. adequate brain/world models;
3. ability to instantiate consciousness on the selected substrate;
4. ability to supply relevant sensory/environmental detail;
5. willingness to allocate resources to ancestor simulation;
6. enough successful simulations to dominate the observer reference class.

A failure at any stage changes the population argument.

## 5. Civilization behavior assumption

The second Bostrom disjunct matters because technological capability does not imply historical behavior.

A mature civilization might:

- prohibit conscious ancestor simulations on ethical grounds;
- lose interest in ancestral history;
- prefer non-conscious historical models;
- use radically compressed or abstract simulations;
- run only a small number of conscious simulations;
- destroy itself or transform into agents with no interest in ancestor worlds.

The argument does not independently establish future simulator preferences.

## 6. Reference-class and indifference assumptions

Population reasoning needs a rule linking observed self-location to a reference class.

Questions include:

- Are we sampled from all conscious observers?
- Human observers only?
- Human-like observer-moments?
- Observers with our approximate memories and evidence?
- Biological and digital observers equally?
- Do short-lived copies count the same as long-lived persons?
- How are duplicated observers weighted?

Brian Weatherson's criticism and Bostrom's reply show that the indifference principle is not a trivial step.

Project judgment:

The simulation argument is not purely a claim about computer engineering. It also depends on anthropic/self-locating inference.

## 7. Simulation nesting

If simulated civilizations themselves run simulations, naive counting can suggest explosive numbers of nested observers.

But resource accounting complicates that intuition.

A lower-level simulation ultimately consumes resources made available by some implementation substrate further up the chain. Nested virtual machines do not automatically create unbounded free computation.

Therefore:

- `nested simulations are conceptually possible` does not imply `arbitrarily many observer levels are physically cheap`;
- observer counting must avoid double-counting computational resources or assuming unlimited recursion depth.

David Wolpert's 2024 treatment supplies a more formal setting for self-simulation/nesting questions, while Bostrom's later debate material addresses stacked simulations conceptually.

## 8. David Kipping's Bayesian reformulation

David Kipping (2020) argues that a Bayesian analysis must include uncertainty about whether Bostrom-like simulations are technically possible.

Under his model averaging, the posterior probability that we are simulated remains below 50 percent and tends toward 50 percent as the number of simulated realities tends toward infinity, absent evidence that civilizations actually produce such simulations.

If humanity eventually creates Bostrom-like conscious simulations, Kipping argues that the odds would shift strongly toward the simulation hypothesis.

Source:
- David Kipping, `A Bayesian Approach to the Simulation Argument` (2020).
- https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.12254

### Important limitation

Kipping's `< 50%` result is not a measurement and is not a universal bound.

It follows from a particular Bayesian treatment of model uncertainty and selected priors/model structure.

- claim type: `BAYESIAN_MODEL`
- evidence class: `SCHOLARLY_INTERPRETATION`

## 9. What would count as new evidence?

Potential evidence that could alter simulation-argument priors includes:

- actually building conscious simulated persons;
- learning that conscious computation is impossible;
- observing advanced civilizations and their simulation behavior;
- discovering hard physical resource constraints that make ancestor simulation impossible;
- discovering implementation artifacts that strongly favor a specific simulation architecture;
- developing a justified observer/reference-class theory that changes self-locating inference.

Technological progress alone is weaker evidence than actual conscious simulation because it does not establish substrate independence or future preferences.

## 10. Alternative models

### A. Extinction / failure before posthumanity

Civilizations rarely reach the necessary technological stage.

This satisfies Bostrom's first disjunct without simulation.

### B. Capability without ancestor-simulation prevalence

Advanced civilizations exist but do not create many conscious ancestor simulations.

This satisfies the second disjunct.

### C. Non-conscious simulations

Advanced civilizations run detailed historical simulations whose inhabitants lack phenomenal consciousness.

This weakens observer-counting conclusions even if the simulations are behaviorally excellent.

### D. Sparse / selective simulation

Simulators generate only the detail needed for selected regions/agents rather than universe-scale fidelity.

This can evade some physical resource objections but introduces new implementation assumptions.

### E. External simulation with radically different parent physics

Physical constraints derived from our universe need not bind a parent universe with different laws.

This makes the hypothesis harder to constrain and therefore less empirically informative.

### F. Base reality with computationally describable laws

Our universe may have digital/computational structure without an external simulator.

This is a major alternative to any argument from `physics looks computational` to `therefore simulation`.

## 11. Falsification / hostile questions

1. What observation would reduce belief in the generic simulation hypothesis if arbitrary simulator choices can explain every observation?
2. If no such observation exists, is the generic hypothesis functioning as metaphysical underdetermination rather than empirical science?
3. Can substrate independence be supported independently of the simulation argument?
4. Can observer counting be defined without controversial reference-class choices?
5. If simulators optimize resources, why assume full-fidelity ancestor worlds rather than non-conscious historical reconstruction?
6. Do nested simulations increase conscious-observer counts in proportion to apparent depth once physical resource accounting is included?
7. Does creating one conscious simulation materially confirm the existence of prior simulators, or only demonstrate possibility?
8. Does a Bayesian prior on simulation covertly encode our technological expectations rather than evidence about reality?

## 12. V1 project position

The simulation argument is philosophically serious and structurally valid enough to warrant detailed study, but its evidential force is highly assumption-sensitive.

V1 therefore does not assign `our universe is simulated` a probability.
