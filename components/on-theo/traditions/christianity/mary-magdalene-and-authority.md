# Mary Magdalene, memory, and authority

## Canonical baseline

The canonical Gospels do **not** identify Mary Magdalene as a prostitute.

They do present her as an important follower associated with Yeshua's final days, death/burial traditions, empty-tomb traditions, and resurrection proclamation. In John, she receives a direct resurrection appearance and is sent to report it to the disciples.

That textual role gives Mary obvious symbolic and authority potential in later Christian memory.

## Noncanonical authority traditions

Several early Christian texts outside the later New Testament canon give Mary unusually prominent interpretive authority.

The *Gospel of Mary* portrays Mary as consoling/instructing other disciples after receiving revelation; Peter challenges the legitimacy of special teaching given to a woman, and Levi defends her.

Other texts also stage Peter/Mary tension or portray Mary as possessing unusual insight or closeness to Jesus.

### Important caution

These works are later than Yeshua's lifetime and reflect the concerns of communities that produced/transmitted them. They do not prove that a historical leadership contest between the actual Peter and Mary occurred exactly as narrated.

The literature **does** show that Mary could function as an authority symbol in competing early Christian constructions of apostolic legitimacy.

## The prostitute conflation

Western Christian tradition eventually fused Mary Magdalene with other female figures, especially the unnamed "sinful woman" of Luke 7 and Mary of Bethany. Gregory the Great's 591 CE homily is a major landmark in consolidating this composite image in the Latin West.

The canonical text itself never says Mary Magdalene practiced prostitution.

## Did the prostitute image deliberately silence her?

Separate three propositions:

1. **TEXTUAL FACT:** canonical sources do not call Mary Magdalene a prostitute.
2. **RECEPTION-HISTORY FACT:** later Western tradition widely portrayed her as a repentant sexual sinner/prostitute through conflation.
3. **MOTIVE CLAIM:** the portrayal was intentionally created to discredit a rival female authority and suppress her counter-account.

The third proposition is plausible within some scholarly interpretations of gender/authority conflict but is not established as a single coordinated historical motive.

A safer formulation is:

> The prostitute/sinner image had the effect of recoding a prominent witness and authority figure primarily as a repentant sexual sinner; whether that effect was the original strategic intent in each stage of the tradition is contested.

## Project relevance

Mary matters to `on-theo` because she is a case study in **transmission and authority filtering**:

- different textual communities assign different levels of authority to the same remembered figure;
- later institutional memory can radically change a figure's dominant public identity;
- a change in reputation can affect which competing traditions audiences consider credible;
- none of this licenses treating every marginalized tradition as automatically more authentic.

The correct question is not "orthodoxy lied, therefore Mary had the secret truth." It is "what textual lineages competed, how did authority get assigned, and what information could have been amplified, transformed, or lost?"
