# Early Islam Material and Documentary Evidence V1

Status: `SOURCE-BASED MATERIAL/DOCUMENTARY PACKET / REVIEW REQUIRED`

## Purpose

Later narrative sources are indispensable for early Islamic history, but they are not the only evidence.

This packet gives independent analytical weight to:

- papyri;
- coins;
- inscriptions;
- graffiti;
- monuments;
- administrative documents;
- dated formulas and titulature.

The aim is not to privilege material evidence automatically, but to keep document date, object date, later literary memory and modern interpretation separate.

## 1. Hoyland's documentary-state problem

Robert Hoyland's 2006 study concentrates especially on the early Islamic state between roughly 1 and 70 AH / 622-690 CE.

He highlights two questions:

1. To what extent did early Muslim rulers continue Byzantine and Sasanian administrative practices versus creating new ones?
2. Why do recognizably Islamic messages become substantially more visible in surviving material evidence under Abd al-Malik (65-85 AH / 685-705)?

Hoyland also stresses the underuse of papyri, coins, inscriptions and comparable documents in reconstructions dominated by literary narratives.

Source:
- Robert Hoyland, `New documentary texts and the early Islamic state`, BSOAS 69.3 (2006), 395-416.
- DOI 10.1017/S0041977X06000188.

## 2. Continuity does not equal unchanged identity

Early conquest administrations inherited functioning fiscal, linguistic and bureaucratic systems.

Continuation of Byzantine or Sasanian administrative forms does not show that the new rulers lacked a distinct religious identity.

Likewise, later administrative Arabization/Islamicization does not prove that all earlier structures were ideologically neutral.

The useful historical question is feature-specific:

- language of documents;
- ruler titles;
- taxation categories;
- coin iconography;
- religious formulas;
- administrative offices;
- calendar/dating systems;
- regional variation.

## 3. Dome of the Rock

The Dome of the Rock is a major dated monumental witness from Abd al-Malik's reign.

The long mosaic inscription is significant because it gives direct public evidence for late seventh-century Islamic theological and political-religious language.

Among its themes are:

- divine unity;
- Muhammad as messenger;
- Jesus/Messiah language;
- rejection of divine sonship/Trinitarian-type formulations;
- Qur'anic or Qur'an-related wording.

V1 does not use the monument as proof that these ideas originated in 691/692. The Qur'an itself is earlier evidence for related propositions.

Instead, the monument shows that such claims were monumentalized in a major imperial/religious building under Abd al-Malik.

## 4. Monument function versus later stories

Christel Kessler's reconsideration is especially useful methodologically because she emphasizes the actual inscription and its message while questioning later narratives that make the Dome a substitute for Meccan pilgrimage during political conflict.

The inscription itself does not mention the Night Journey or present the Rock explicitly through the later mi'raj framework.

Project guard:

`later explanation of why monument was built` != `contemporary inscriptional statement of why it was built`.

## 5. Coins

Coinage can preserve:

- ruler names/titles;
- mint/date information;
- iconographic continuity/change;
- religious formulas;
- administrative geography.

Early Islamic coinage often develops through adaptation of Byzantine/Sasanian prototypes before more explicitly aniconic/epigraphic Islamic forms become prominent under Abd al-Malik.

V1 guard:

Coin reform is evidence for changing state representation and monetary policy; it is not by itself a timestamp for the invention of Islamic belief.

## 6. Papyri

Papyri can provide unusually direct evidence for ordinary administration and society:

- receipts;
- tax demands;
- official letters;
- contracts;
- multilingual/bilingual administrative practice;
- names/titles/formulas.

Their evidentiary value differs from theological literature.

A tax papyrus can tell us much about government practice and little directly about detailed theology unless religious formulas appear in the document.

## 7. Graffiti and rock inscriptions

Graffiti and short inscriptions can preserve religious formulas, names, prayers and dates outside major elite literary corpora.

But they require exact epigraphic context.

Project guard:

- transcription != artifact autopsy;
- undated graffiti cannot receive a precise chronology merely because its formula resembles a later dated text;
- formula occurrence does not imply all later theology associated with that formula.

## 8. Documentary silence

The relative scarcity or absence of a formula in surviving early evidence must be handled carefully.

Possible explanations include:

- formula genuinely not yet widespread;
- different medium conventions;
- survival bias;
- regional practice;
- administrative conservatism;
- sample size;
- later destruction/recycling;
- discovery/publication bias.

Therefore:

`not yet found in surviving documents` != `did not exist`.

Conversely, repeated securely dated appearance after a particular period can still be evidence of increased public use.

## 9. Evidence clocks

Every material claim should separate:

1. event/reign being discussed;
2. artifact production date;
3. inscription/document text date if separately established;
4. modern discovery/publication date;
5. later interpretation date.

The Dome of the Rock is particularly useful because the building/inscription can be historically dated near the event context rather than known only from much later literature.

## 10. Proposed witness/source model

Future registry entries should distinguish:

- `MAT-DOME-ROCK-INSCRIPTION-691-692`
- individual coin types or coin specimens where a specific reading matters;
- named papyri by collection/inventory number;
- named graffiti/rock inscriptions.

A generic work/source node should not inherit the exact physical date/readings of every artifact.

## 11. Comparative use

Material evidence is especially valuable when compared with literary traditions.

Examples:

- later chronicles explain a reform; coins show what actually changes and when;
- later biographies preserve ruler ideology; inscriptions show contemporary official wording;
- administrative narratives describe policy; papyri show local implementation.

Agreement strengthens historical reconstruction.

Disagreement should remain visible rather than being automatically harmonized.

## 12. V1 project position

The early Islamic material record supports a model of **development and increasing public articulation**, especially around Abd al-Malik, rather than either extreme:

- `everything later associated with Islam was fully institutionally fixed in 632`, or
- `recognizable Islam was suddenly invented under Abd al-Malik`.

The evidence currently supports a more granular question: which public, administrative, textual and theological forms become visible when, in which media, and in which regions?
