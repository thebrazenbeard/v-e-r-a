## Historical scope

This packet begins after the Qur'anic internal-system reconstruction and asks how religious, political, legal, textual, and confessional systems developed across the first centuries of Islam.

`Early Islam` is not treated as one finished institution present in complete form at Muhammad's death.

V1 separates at least these evidence layers:

1. Qur'anic text and its own internal religious system.
2. Earliest external/non-Muslim testimonies and documentary evidence.
3. Papyri, coins, inscriptions, monuments, administrative documents and graffiti.
4. Early Arabic historical writing and sira-maghazi traditions.
5. Hadith collection, isnad criticism and later canonical corpora.
6. Judges, legal specialists, legal theory and formation of legal schools.
7. Political succession disputes and the later formation of Sunni and Shi'i identities.
8. Umayyad and Abbasid state/institutional developments.

Later evidence can preserve earlier material, but later date does not disappear merely because a report concerns an earlier event.

## Qur'an versus later Islamic tradition

The Qur'an is primary evidence for Qur'anic propositions.

It does not contain a complete later biography of Muhammad, a fully developed hadith canon, the mature Sunni legal schools, Twelver Shi'i imamology, Abbasid institutions, or later kalam systems.

Those developments require their own sources and chronology.

This does not imply they are inventions without earlier roots. It means the roots and the later system are different historical claims.

## Muhammad and early biography

Sean W. Anthony's *Muhammad and the Empires of Faith* rejects the idea that historians face a simple choice between accepting or discarding Ibn Ishaq/Ibn Hisham as one late bottleneck.

Anthony's source program distinguishes:

- early non-Muslim testimonies;
- material earlier than the surviving full sira;
- early preaching and narrative motifs;
- Umayyad beginnings of sira-maghazi literature;
- material associated with Urwa ibn al-Zubayr and other pre-Ibn-Ishaq transmitters;
- later redaction and narrative development.

This permits historical analysis without treating every later biography either as transparent eyewitness record or as worthless fiction.

Sources:
- Sean W. Anthony, *Muhammad and the Empires of Faith*.
- https://www.ucpress.edu/books/muhammad-and-the-empires-of-faith/epub-pdf
- https://www.ucpress.edu/blog-posts/51668-the-empires-of-faith-and-the-making-of-the-prophet-of-islam

## Conquest and state formation

The polity that expanded after Muhammad's death inherited and transformed territories previously governed by Byzantine and Sasanian systems.

Robert Hoyland's documentary work emphasizes that early Islamic-state reconstruction should not rely solely on later chronicles. Papyri, coins, rock inscriptions and other documents bear directly on administration and ideology.

Hoyland frames an important historical problem for roughly 1-70 AH / 622-690 CE:

- which Byzantine/Sasanian practices continued;
- which practices were changed or newly introduced;
- why explicitly recognizable Islamic messages become substantially clearer in the material record under Abd al-Malik.

This is not evidence that Islam did not exist before Abd al-Malik. It is evidence that public state representation, documentary habits and ideological signaling developed historically.

Source:
- Robert Hoyland, `New documentary texts and the early Islamic state`, BSOAS 69.3 (2006), 395-416.
- DOI 10.1017/S0041977X06000188.

## Abd al-Malik and public Islamic articulation

The Dome of the Rock, completed under Abd al-Malik and conventionally dated 72/691-692, provides unusually important monumental epigraphy.

Its long Qur'anic/monotheist inscriptions offer direct material-textual evidence for late seventh-century public Islamic claims, including language about God, Muhammad and Jesus.

The monument must be interpreted from its actual inscriptional content rather than from later stories alone about why it was built.

Christel Kessler's inscriptional study stresses the importance of the monument's dated text and the need to distinguish it from later interpretive traditions such as claims about replacing Meccan pilgrimage.

Sources:
- Christel Kessler, `Abd al-Malik's Inscription in the Dome of the Rock: A Reconsideration`.
- Sheila Blair and Jonathan Bloom, `Inscriptions in art and architecture`, *Cambridge Companion to the Qur'an*.

## Hadith is a historical corpus with a formation history

Hadith traditions preserve reports attributed to Muhammad and other early authorities.

But the extant hadith corpus has its own collection, transmission, criticism, forgery, isnad and canonization history.

Oxford Bibliographies summarizes scholarly study of hadith collection and authenticity across approximately 632-900 CE and notes both Muslim isnad criticism and modern historical debates over reliability and elaboration.

Project guards:

- `hadith says X happened during Muhammad's lifetime` != `we possess a written eyewitness document from Muhammad's lifetime`;
- later collection != automatic fabrication;
- isnad existence != automatic authentication;
- source criticism must proceed report by report/tradition cluster by tradition cluster.

Source:
- Oxford Bibliographies, `Hadith`, section on early collection and authenticity.

## Law did not arrive as finished madhhabs

Wael Hallaq reconstructs a long development from Quranic law and early legal ethic through judges, legal specialists, prophetic authority, legal reasoning, legal theory and eventual formation of legal schools.

This structure itself is historically important: later madhhabs and developed usul al-fiqh should not be projected unchanged into the first generation.

Early qadis also combined administrative, judicial and other functions differently from the later mature judiciary.

Source:
- Wael B. Hallaq, *The Origins and Evolution of Islamic Law*.

## Succession and confessional development

Muhammad's death generated real political and religious disputes over legitimate leadership.

Those disputes are important ancestors of later Sunni and Shi'i identity, but later confessional systems should not simply be projected backward.

Toby Matthiesen emphasizes both:

- the early succession dispute around caliphate, Ali and the Prophet's family;
- the long historical formation of Sunnism and Shiism, including periods of ambiguity, overlap, convergence and polarization.

His explicit historical warning is valuable for V1: the further back one goes, the less later Sunni/Shi'i categories map cleanly onto early groups.

Source:
- Toby Matthiesen, *The Caliph and the Imam: The Making of Sunnism and Shiism* (2023).

## Umayyad and Abbasid layers

The Umayyad and Abbasid periods should not be treated merely as secular dynastic backgrounds to an already completed religion.

They are environments in which:

- public religious language develops;
- historical memory is collected and contested;
- administrative systems change;
- legal specialists gain authority;
- hadith networks expand;
- theological disputes sharpen;
- sectarian/confessional boundaries evolve;
- Arabic literary and scholarly institutions expand.

State patronage, opposition to state power, local scholarly networks and transregional circulation all matter.

## V1 source hierarchy

For a seventh-century claim, V1 prioritizes evidence by relevance rather than by one blanket ranking:

- a dated seventh-century inscription is excellent evidence for what that inscription says and for its public context;
- a papyrus is strong evidence for its administrative/social transaction;
- the Qur'an is primary for Qur'anic discourse;
- a later historical narrative may preserve early traditions but requires transmission/redaction analysis;
- a ninth-century hadith collection is primary evidence for the collected tradition in that form and possible evidence for earlier material only after source criticism.

`earlier` does not automatically mean `truer`, and `later` does not automatically mean `false`.

## Major V1 development axes

### Authority

Prophetic authority -> companions/family -> caliph/imam claims -> scholars/transmitters/judges -> juristic schools and theological institutions.

This arrow is a research sequence, not a claim of linear replacement.

### Text

Qur'an -> recitation/manuscript history -> historical reports -> hadith corpora -> tafsir/legal/theological corpora.

Each textual family develops on a different timeline.

### State

early community -> conquest polity -> Umayyad imperial formation -> Abd al-Malik reforms/public articulation -> Abbasid transformation.

Again, boundaries are analytical rather than instantaneous.

### Confession

succession disputes -> Alid loyalties/oppositions/other early groupings -> multiple Shi'i trajectories and evolving Sunni formations.

The mature binary is later than the initial dispute.
