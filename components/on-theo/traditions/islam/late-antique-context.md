# Qur'an in Late Antique context

## Historical placement

The Qur'an is the most important surviving Arabic prose text from seventh-century Arabia and should be studied inside the religiously plural world of Late Antiquity rather than as though it appeared in a vacuum.

Its discourse assumes familiarity with figures and themes known from Jewish and Christian traditions while frequently retelling them elliptically rather than introducing them from scratch.

Modern scholarship increasingly studies these relationships as more than a hunt for one-to-one "borrowings." Relevant possibilities include:

- direct textual or oral dependence;
- liturgical/homiletic circulation;
- Jewish and Christian communities in or near Arabia;
- noncanonical Christian traditions;
- Jewish interpretive traditions;
- shared Late Antique narrative environments;
- deliberate Qur'anic correction/polemic against positions attributed to Jews or Christians.

## Time separation from Yeshua

For events attributed to first-century Yeshua/Jesus:

- `claimed_event_date`: first century CE;
- `Qur'anic source date`: seventh century CE.

This time gap is always material.

A Qur'anic claim may preserve older tradition, reject older tradition, creatively refract it, or address seventh-century religious controversy. It cannot simply be counted as another first-century eyewitness vote.

## Judaism and Christianity as living interlocutors

The Qur'an presents itself in continuity with earlier revelation while also criticizing communities for theological/error claims. Consequently its retellings often function as arguments, not neutral antiquarian reports.

For `on-theo`, that makes the Qur'an especially useful for studying **what conceptual conflicts remained alive centuries later**:

- divine unity;
- sonship;
- Jesus's status;
- Mary's purity;
- revelation and prophetic continuity;
- crucifixion/death;
- resurrection;
- judgment;
- corrupted or misunderstood authority.

## Noncanonical parallels

Some Qur'anic narratives parallel material found outside the canonical New Testament, including infancy traditions. This does not require direct copying from a surviving manuscript; oral, liturgical and transformed narrative circulation must remain possible.

## Project relevance

The Qur'an is simultaneously:

1. too late to function as first-century biography;
2. early enough to preserve a major Late Antique state of the Abrahamic argument;
3. structurally rich for comparison because it often preserves earlier figures while changing their theological role.

That combination is exactly what the commonality and chronology branches need.
