## 1. Ordinary burial archaeology

At Ur, the famous royal graves are numerically exceptional.

Penn Museum's modern overview of the Royal Cemetery reports more than 2,000 graves, of which only 16 had the structure, wealth, and associated retainer burials that led Woolley to classify them as royal. The vast majority were much simpler rectangular pit burials with few offerings.

Controls:

ROYAL_CEMETERY_NAME != MOST_BURIALS_WERE_ROYAL

ELITE_RETAINER_RITE != NORMAL_SUMERIAN_FUNERAL

Material implication:
- ordinary inhumation must be modeled separately from exceptional elite/royal mortuary display;
- grave wealth, architecture, attendants, and ritual equipment vary sharply;
- a model built only from Puabi, PG 789, or PG 1237 would badly overfit elite evidence.

Sources:
- Penn Museum, William B. Hafford, "A Spectacular Discovery: Burials Simple and Splendid"
  https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/a-spectacular-discovery/
- Penn Museum, "Ur and Its Treasures"
  https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/ur-and-its-treasures/

Classification:

## 2. Exceptional retainer burials at Early Dynastic Ur

The Early Dynastic III Royal Cemetery preserves exceptional burials in which numerous attendants were interred in association with elite tombs/death pits.

Earlier interpretations that the attendants calmly drank poison are not secure.

Modern CT-based work on two preserved skulls found evidence consistent with lethal blunt-force trauma and intentional corpse preservation involving heat and mercury/cinnabar treatment.

This supports:
- at least some attendants were violently killed;
- funerary preparation could be prolonged and elaborate;
- Woolley's voluntary-poison model cannot stand as the default explanation.

It does NOT support:
- that every attendant in every royal grave died by the same mechanism;
- that every retainer burial was a sacrifice to a god;
- that all Sumerian rulers were buried with human victims;
- that the practice continued unchanged into Ur III.

Source:
- Aubrey Baadsgaard, Janet Monge, Samantha Cox, Richard L. Zettler,
  "Human sacrifice and intentional corpse preservation in the Royal Cemetery of Ur,"
  Antiquity 85 (2011): 27–42.
  https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003598X00067417

Supporting institutional summary:
- Penn, Richard L. Zettler, "Interpreting Iraqi Artifacts Through Context and Association"
  https://almanac.upenn.edu/archive/volumes/v56/n09/zettler.html

Classification:
- CT/pathology of examined remains: MATERIAL_EVIDENCE

## 3. Royal literary afterlife: Ur-Namma

### Composition/witness boundary

The Death of Ur-Namma is a Sumerian literary composition about the death and underworld fate of the Ur III founder.

The available literary witnesses are much later than Ur-Namma's reign. ETCSL's bibliography lists manuscripts from Nippur and Susa, and ISAC notes tablets dating around the early second millennium BCE.

Therefore:

UR_NAMMA_LITERARY_AFTERLIFE != CONTEMPORARY_URIII_FUNERAL_REPORT

The text is primary evidence for a Sumerian literary tradition, not a stenographic report of the king's actual burial.

Sources:
- ETCSL, The Death of Ur-Namma (Ur-Namma A)
  https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.2.4.1.1
- ETCSL bibliography
  https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section2/b2411.htm
- ISAC, Treasures from the Royal Tombs of Ur exhibit text
  https://isac.uchicago.edu/museum-exhibits/special-exhibits/treasures-royal-tombs-ur-2

### What the text depicts

The composition gives Ur-Namma an organized postmortem itinerary:
- movement toward the netherworld;
- burial of donkeys with him in the literary account;
- offerings to underworld gatekeepers and deities;
- slaughter/feasting and gifts;
- bitter food and brackish water as netherworld imagery;
- assignment of a dwelling/place/status in the underworld;
- a judicial role associated with Gilgamesh in at least one textual sequence.

This is strong evidence that Sumerian literary tradition could imagine:
- a socially organized netherworld;
- divine authorities there;
- offerings having relevance to postmortem status;
- differentiated royal fate.

It does NOT establish:
- that ordinary dead received Ur-Namma's privileges;
- that his actual grave contained every item described;
- one universal funerary liturgy;
- a stable doctrine unchanged across Sumerian history.

Classification:
- textual occurrence: PRIMARY_TEXT
- actual Ur III funerary reconstruction from the composition: UNKNOWN unless independently corroborated.

## 4. Royal mortality and ghost status: The Death of Gilgamesh

The Death of Gilgamesh explicitly treats death as unavoidable for humans despite Gilgamesh's extraordinary status.

The composition portrays him as becoming an important ghost/underworld authority rather than escaping mortality.

It also connects memory, descendants, funerary statues, and the continued naming of the dead.

Source:
- ETCSL, The Death of Gilgamesh
  https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.1.8.1.3

Current bounded reconstruction:
- death is presented as universal for humans;
- exceptional royal status may continue in the netherworld;
- memory and commemoration matter;
- heroic/royal afterlife is differentiated rather than simple annihilation.

Guards:

GILGAMESH_POSTMORTEM_AUTHORITY != ORDINARY_PERSON_DEFAULT_FATE

LITERARY_FUNERARY_STATUE_REFERENCE != ARCHAEOLOGICAL_PROOF_OF_UNIVERSAL_STATUE_PRACTICE

## 5. Ordinary ghosts, descendants, and funerary offerings

Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld is the strongest V1 Sumerian literary source for differentiated conditions among the dead.

The text has Gilgamesh question Enkidu about the "order of the netherworld."

The answers vary according to circumstances including:
- number of sons/descendants;
- absence of an heir;
- manner of death;
- whether funerary offerings are received;
- stillbirth;
- burning/cremation.

Especially important:
the spirit of a person with no funerary offerings is depicted as eating discarded scraps/crumbs.

Source:
- ETCSL, Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld
  https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.1.8.1.4

This supports a literary model in which:
- the dead remain socially situated;
- living descendants can matter to postmortem welfare;
- funerary provisioning matters;
- not all dead share one identical condition.

It does NOT justify treating the entire list as a census of empirically held beliefs among every Sumerian community.

Classification:
- individual textual propositions: PRIMARY_TEXT
- generalized social theology: HISTORICAL_RECONSTRUCTION

High-value control:

AFTERLIFE_CONDITION = DIFFERENTIATED_IN_TEXT

not:

ONE_UNIFORM_SUMERIAN_AFTERLIFE

## 6. Family care for the dead

Dina Katz's "Sumerian Funerary Rituals in Context" synthesizes literary, ritual, and comparative Mesopotamian evidence around continuing care for dead family members.

The core pattern is:
- the dead continue as a spirit/ghost;
- burial manages the transition from corpse to socially recognized dead;
- regular food/drink offerings maintain the dead;
- kinship obligation continues after death.

Source:
- Dina Katz, "Sumerian Funerary Rituals in Context," in Nicola Laneri (ed.),
  Performing Death: Social Analyses of Funerary Traditions in the Ancient Near East and Mediterranean,
  Oriental Institute Seminars 3 (2007), pp. 167–188.
  https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/ois3.pdf

Important chronological guard:
Katz's evidence is not limited to one city, one century, or exclusively third-millennium texts. It is a scholarly reconstruction across a wider evidentiary field.

Therefore:

FAMILY_OFFERING_SYSTEM = STRONG_MESOPOTAMIAN_PATTERN

but exact Sumerian-period claims must remain source- and date-bounded.

## 7. Ghosts as a broader Mesopotamian category

Modern scholarship on Mesopotamian ghosts describes dead persons as retaining identity and needs such as food, water, and family attention. Improper burial or neglected rites could be associated with restless/problematic ghosts.

However, the evidentiary corpus is chronologically uneven: first-millennium Babylonian and Assyrian ritual/omen material is much richer than the surviving early Sumerian evidence.

Source:
- Oxford, "ghosts, Mesopotamia"
  https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/550533662

This is used here as a diachronic control, not as permission to back-project every later ghost ritual into Early Dynastic Sumer.

Rule:

LATER_MESOPOTAMIAN_GHOST_SYSTEM != AUTOMATIC_EARLY_SUMERIAN_DOCTRINE

## 8. Divine descent remains a separate lane

Inana's Descent to the Netherworld is evidence for:
- divine underworld geography/authority;
- divine vulnerability and restoration;
- ritual/lament motifs;
- relations among deities.

It is not the primary control for ordinary human postmortem fate.

The existing packet's separation remains correct:

INANA_DESCENT != HUMAN_AFTERLIFE_MANUAL

The human-death lane should privilege:
- funerary archaeology;
- human ghost/dead texts;
- royal death literature explicitly about deceased humans;
- family mortuary ritual;
- burial and offering evidence.

## 10. Ur Royal Cemetery: what can and cannot be inferred

### Supported

- the cemetery contained thousands of burials over time;
- most were materially simpler than the small set Woolley called royal;
- a tiny elite subset had extraordinary wealth and associated retainers;
- at least two examined retainers show evidence consistent with violent killing;
- corpse preservation/treatment was used in at least some elite funerary contexts;
- elite funerals could mobilize large numbers of people, objects, animals, music, feasting, and display.

### Not established from this evidence alone

- universal Sumerian human sacrifice;
- voluntary death of retainers;
- a single mechanism of killing;
- exact beliefs held by every attendant;
- that royal death pits directly instantiate the later Ur-Namma or Gilgamesh literary descriptions;
- that ordinary Sumerians expected equivalent burial or afterlife status.

Project classification:

ROYAL_CEMETERY = EXCEPTIONAL_ELITE_MORTUARY_SYSTEM

not:

ROYAL_CEMETERY = DEFAULT_SUMERIAN_AFTERLIFE

## 11. Diachronic model V1

The strongest bounded reconstruction is:

Sumerian and early Mesopotamian evidence treats human death as a socially consequential transition rather than simple disappearance. The dead could remain relevant as ghosts/spirits; burial, descendants, offerings, memory, and circumstances of death could affect the represented condition of the dead. Elite/royal traditions could imagine differentiated postmortem status and could involve extraordinary mortuary display or retainer burial.

This is HISTORICAL_RECONSTRUCTION.

It does not imply:
- one canon of afterlife doctrine;
- moral judgment resembling later heaven/hell systems;
- resurrection as ordinary Sumerian expectation;
- identical treatment of elite and non-elite dead;
- timeless uniformity from Early Dynastic through Old Babylonian and later Mesopotamian traditions.
