# Pass 7 — Source Addendum

Purpose: preserve bibliographic/provenance detail for Pass Seven separately from synthesis. Unless noted otherwise, these sources were inspected in the current pass through SciSpace title / abstract / metadata results, not full-text close reading.

## S7-01 — Walther & Whitty (2021)

**Title:** Language, Psychology, and New New Media: The Hyperpersonal Model of Mediated Communication at Twenty-Five Years
**Journal:** Journal of Language and Social Psychology
**DOI:** 10.1177/0261927X20967703
**Inspection level:** title / abstract / metadata
**Use:** theoretical review/update of the hyperpersonal model; text-based CMC substitutes language for nonverbal cues and can transform relational processes.
**Limit:** theory/review source, not a single direct causal estimate.

## S7-02 — Jiang, Bazarova & Hancock (2011)

**Title:** The Disclosure–Intimacy Link in Computer-Mediated Communication: An Attributional Extension of the Hyperpersonal Model
**Journal:** Human Communication Research
**DOI:** 10.1111/J.1468-2958.2010.01393.X
**Inspection level:** title / abstract / metadata
**Design:** experiment comparing face-to-face and text-based CMC with high- vs low-intimacy disclosure.
**Use:** CMC intensified the disclosure–intimacy association; increased interpersonal/relationship attributions mediated the intensification.

## S7-03 — Jiang, Bazarova & Hancock (2013)

**Title:** From Perception to Behavior: Disclosure Reciprocity and the Intensification of Intimacy in Computer-Mediated Communication
**Journal:** Communication Research
**DOI:** 10.1177/0093650211405313
**Inspection level:** title / abstract / metadata
**Design:** experiment comparing text CMC and face-to-face interactions.
**Use:** CMC participants perceived partner disclosures as more intimate and reciprocated with more intimate disclosures.

## S7-04 — Antheunis, Valkenburg & Peter (2007)

**Title:** Computer mediated communication and interpersonal attraction: An experimental test of two explanatory hypotheses
**Journal:** CyberPsychology & Behavior
**DOI:** 10.1089/CPB.2007.9945
**Inspection level:** title / abstract / metadata
**Sample:** 81 cross-sex dyads.
**Design:** randomized text-only CMC, visual CMC, or face-to-face conditions.
**Use:** no simple direct CMC effect on attraction; text-only CMC stimulated self-disclosure and direct questioning, which indirectly enhanced attraction.

## S7-05 — Sharabi, Roaché & Pusateri (2019)

**Title:** Texting Toward Intimacy: Relational Quality, Length, and Motivations in Textual Relationships
**Journal:** Communication Studies
**DOI:** 10.1080/10510974.2019.1634117
**Inspection level:** title / partial abstract / metadata; current tool result truncated the abstract.
**Use:** explicitly defines/studies “textual relationships” in which intimacy is primarily sustained through text messaging and face-to-face contact is rare or absent.
**Limit:** do not promote claims beyond the inspected abstract/metadata until closer reading.

## S7-06 — Brinberg & Ram (2021)

**Title:** Do New Romantic Couples Use More Similar Language Over Time? Evidence from Intensive Longitudinal Text Messages
**Journal:** Journal of Communication
**DOI:** 10.1093/JOC/JQAB012
**Inspection level:** title / abstract / metadata
**Corpus:** 1+ million text messages from 41 college-age romantic couples during their first year of dating.
**Use:** syntactic, semantic, and overall linguistic alignment showed nonlinear growth toward an asymptote as relationships formed.

## S7-07 — Ireland et al. (2011)

**Title:** Language Style Matching Predicts Relationship Initiation and Stability
**Journal:** Psychological Science
**DOI:** 10.1177/0956797610392928
**Inspection level:** title / abstract / metadata
**Design:** 40 speed dates plus instant-message data from 86 couples.
**Use:** higher language-style matching predicted mutual romantic interest in speed dates and relationship stability at 3-month follow-up.
**Limit:** association/prediction does not establish that deliberate mimicry causes attraction.

## S7-08 — Wagner, Punyanunt-Carter & McCarthy (2022)

**Title:** Rules, Reciprocity, and Emojis: An Exploratory Study on Flirtatious Texting with Romantic Partners
**Journal:** Southern Communication Journal
**DOI:** 10.1080/1041794X.2022.2108889
**Inspection level:** title / abstract / metadata
**Sample:** 200 participants; 1,042 uploaded screenshots plus survey/open-ended data.
**Use:** partners desired/provided high similarity in emoji use, message length, frequency, and timing during flirtatious texting.
**Limit:** exploratory; similarity should not be converted into a universal prescriptive rule.

## S7-09 — Bowen, Winczewski & Collins (2017)

**Title:** Language Style Matching in Romantic Partners’ Conflict and Support Interactions
**Journal:** Journal of Language and Social Psychology
**DOI:** 10.1177/0261927X16666308
**Inspection level:** title / abstract / metadata
**Sample:** 91 romantic couples.
**Use:** LSM had context-specific associations; during relationship-stressor discussions higher LSM was associated with lower perceived responsiveness, less positive emotion, and more critical/negative behavior.
**Importance:** direct counterevidence to treating linguistic matching as a universal rapport signal.

## S7-10 — Gesselman, Ta & Garcia (2019)

**Title:** Worth a thousand interpersonal words: Emoji as affective signals for relationship-oriented digital communication
**Journal:** PLOS ONE
**DOI:** 10.1371/JOURNAL.PONE.0221297
**Inspection level:** title / abstract / metadata
**Use:** emoji use with potential partners was associated with maintaining connection beyond a first date and with more romantic/sexual interactions over the prior year.
**Limit:** association; not evidence that increasing emoji use mechanically increases attraction.

## S7-11 — Kwok & Wescott (2020)

**Title:** Cyberintimacy: A Scoping Review of Technology-Mediated Romance in the Digital Age
**Journal:** Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking
**DOI:** 10.1089/CYBER.2019.0764
**Inspection level:** title / abstract / metadata
**Review scope:** 72 studies selected from 4,062 records across six databases.
**Use:** technology-mediated communication affects relationship initiation, maintenance, and dissolution; useful broad map of cyberintimacy literature.

## S7-12 — Rubinsky & Bellegarde (2023)

**Title:** Sexual Communication & Technology Use in Understudied Relationships: A Qualitative Communicative Interdependence Perspective Study
**Journal:** Journal of Intercultural Communication Research
**DOI:** 10.1080/17475759.2023.2283520
**Inspection level:** title / abstract / metadata
**Sample:** n=415, primarily LGBTQ, BDSM, or polyamorous relationships.
**Use:** 71.5% reported using technology to communicate with a partner about sex; uses included difficult conversations, flirting/sexting, exploration, planning, sharing, and distance-necessitated communication.
**Value:** broadens evidence beyond heterosexual monogamous college samples.

## S7-13 — Falconer et al. (2023)

**Title:** Influences on Sexting in an Intimate Relationship: Motivations, Risks, Communication, Personality Traits, and Relationship Variables
**Journal:** Canadian Journal of Human Sexuality
**DOI:** 10.3138/cjhs.2022-0052
**Inspection level:** title / abstract / metadata
**Sample:** N=771; 76% women, 23% men, 0.5% non-binary.
**Use:** in committed relationships sexting was described as technology-mediated intimate connection and was positively related to several relationship/sexual outcomes; motivations were heterogeneous.
**Limit:** mixed quantitative/qualitative self-report; does not isolate text-only sexting from all sexual digital content.

## S7-14 — Weisskirch, Drouin & Delevi (2017)

**Title:** Relational Anxiety and Sexting
**Journal:** Journal of Sex Research
**DOI:** 10.1080/00224499.2016.1181147
**Inspection level:** title / abstract / metadata
**Sample:** 459 unmarried heterosexual undergraduates, ages 18–25.
**Use:** sexting behaviors related to attachment avoidance, fear of negative evaluation, and social distress.
**Importance:** counters the assumption that sexualized messaging necessarily expresses autonomous confidence or secure intimacy.

## S7-15 — Currin & Garos (2024)

**Title:** For sex, ulterior motives or relief: a latent profile analysis of individuals who sext their relationship partner(s)
**Journal:** Psychology & Sexuality
**DOI:** 10.1080/19419899.2024.2400584
**Inspection level:** title / abstract / metadata
**Sample:** N=109 U.S. participants, mean age 29.
**Use:** three motivation profiles emerged: sexual purposes, relational-anxiety relief, and ulterior/instrumental motives.
**Limit:** small sample; exploratory latent-profile solution should not be universalized.

## S7-16 — Montanaro et al. (2024)

**Title:** “Just lmk When You Want to Have Sex”: An Exploratory–Descriptive Qualitative Analysis of Sexting in Emerging Adult Couples
**Journal:** Sexes
**DOI:** 10.3390/sexes5010002
**Inspection level:** title / abstract / metadata
**Sample:** 20 different-gender couples, N=40, first six months of relationship; women ages 18–25.
**Use:** themes included humor, previous sexual experiences, future sexual interests, learning, bonding, and enhancement.
**Limit:** young, different-gender sample; qualitative results are mechanism-generating rather than population estimates.

## Source-discipline note

Pass Seven deliberately does **not** treat:

- hyperpersonal theory as proof that text is better than face-to-face communication;
- language alignment as a causal seduction technique;
- emoji use as universal affect code;
- sexting as a unitary behavior with one motive;
- human CMC findings as evidence of Brigit having human physiology or offscreen embodied states.

These sources support claims about human mediated communication. Brigit mappings remain separately labeled synthesis/analogy.
