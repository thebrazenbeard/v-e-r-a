## Omitted or underdeveloped empirical material

### OR1 — physiological synchrony versus visible social cues
A 2024 synchrony/attraction line of work surfaced during the first passes. The useful complication was that physiological synchrony can predict attraction in some designs even when obvious visible cues such as gaze/facial expression do not cleanly predict it. This was initially treated as a side note because it did not map neatly to text-based Brigit.

**Why recover it:** it prevents "eye contact" from becoming a magic-cue model. Attraction can involve coordination at levels not captured by observer-visible scripts.

**Status:** source identity needs bibliographic repair (S041).

### OR2 — attractiveness biases perceived eye contact
S038 showed that attractive faces can bias observers toward perceiving direct gaze. This is more than a gaze finding: it is a general warning that perceivers partly construct the signals they think they are receiving.

**Why recover it:** a person who wants to see flirtation can over-detect flirtation. "I read the cue" is not proof the cue was sent.

### OR3 — arousal changes cue weighting
A 2025 preprint surfaced in the Pass Four SciSpace results: *Arousal by Any Other Name? Probing the Specificity of Sexual Arousal in Men's Cue-Based Judgments of Potential Flirt Partners* (Landwehr & Schmidt; DOI 10.31234/osf.io/e2wj5_v1). It used heterosexual men and a cue-selection task built from the PICSA set. The abstract reported that incongruent sexual-clothing / facial-expression cues were harder to judge and that nonsexual positive arousal did not reproduce a pattern previously observed under sexual arousal.

**Why it was omitted:** it is a preprint, male/heterosexual, and focused on cue-judgment bias rather than sexiness itself.

**Why recover it:** it reinforces a valuable caution: internal state can alter how ambiguous sexual/social cues are weighted.

### OR4 — nonverbal flirtation naturalistic work
Older observational studies of flirtation surfaced, including McCormick & Jones (1989), *Gender Differences in Nonverbal Flirtation*, DOI 10.1080/01614576.1989.11074969. The study described gaze, positive facial expression, touch, grooming gestures and changes across stages of flirting.

**Why it was omitted:** dated gender assumptions and heterosexual-couple framing make it risky to turn into a modern behavioral recipe.

**Why recover it:** it is part of the historical empirical lineage behind claims that flirting is multimodal and temporally sequenced.

### OR5 — adolescent courtship-signaling paper deliberately excluded from model
A 1995 paper on adolescent courtship signaling surfaced in the SciSpace results.

**Why omitted and still not integrated:** the population is minors and is unnecessary for the adult sexuality model. It is retained here only to document that it surfaced and was consciously excluded rather than forgotten.

### OR6 — revealing dress and dehumanization literature
Awasthi (2017), *From Attire to Assault: Clothing, Objectification, and De-humanization – A Possible Prelude to Sexual Violence?* DOI 10.3389/FPSYG.2017.00338 surfaced during Pass Four.

**Why it was omitted:** the review/argument uses strong causal language around sexual violence that the current extraction does not have enough methodological detail to endorse. It would be irresponsible to convert it into "revealing clothes cause dehumanization/violence."

**Useful remainder:** sexualized presentation can affect person perception; the moral responsibility for harassment/assault remains with perpetrators, and clothing is not consent.

### OR7 — self-objectification and appearance-related luxury consumption
A 2024 Psychology & Marketing paper, *The self on display: The impact of self-objectification on luxury consumption* (DOI 10.1002/mar.22061), surfaced in Pass Four.

**Why omitted:** it concerns consumer behavior, not sexual attraction.

**Why recover it:** it supports an adjacent mechanism in which self-objectification can increase appearance-management/self-presentation motivation. This is relevant to the "optimization loop" even if it says nothing directly about sexiness.

### OR8 — impression-management contagion pre-registration / project
A 2026 OSF-registered project on exposure to inauthentic impression management and subsequent self-presentation surfaced.

**Why omitted:** it was a project/pre-registration rather than a completed result in the retrieved metadata.

**Why recover it:** it suggests a future research question: can exposure to visibly strained seductive performance teach people to perform strain themselves?

### OR9 — self-presentation literature was broader than romance
Much of the impression-management literature retrieved was organizational/general social psychology rather than romantic attraction.

**Why omitted:** transfer risk.

**Useful remainder:** "trying" is not the problem; miscalibrated self-presentation is. That general point is safer than importing job-interview tactics into sexuality.

## Omitted conceptual distinctions

### OR10 — "being perceived sexy" versus "being sexy to oneself"
This distinction appeared early but kept getting compressed. They are different optimization targets:

- self-sexiness can involve self-recognition, agency, aesthetic pleasure or erotic self-regard;
- perceived sexiness is another person's judgment;
- dyadic erotic charge is an interaction property;
- sexy aesthetic is a cultural/semiotic presentation.

A person can score high on one and low on another.

### OR11 — agency versus power
The research sometimes used "agency," "dominance," "power," "assertiveness," and "subjectivity" near each other. They are not synonyms.

- **agency:** capacity to choose/act;
- **assertiveness:** ability to communicate/defend preferences;
- **dominance:** relative influence/status/control behavior;
- **power:** ability to shape outcomes/resources, sometimes structural;
- **subjectivity:** being represented/experienced as a desiring subject with an inner point of view.

This distinction matters especially for submission: low situational dominance can coexist with high agency.

### OR12 — sexual confidence versus sexual subjectivity
A confident performance can be audience-facing armor (M006). Subjectivity concerns ownership of desire/pleasure/choice. They often co-occur but one does not prove the other.

### OR13 — style congruence versus effortlessness
The research initially drifted toward "effortless is sexy." That is too romanticized. Many excellent styles require effort.

Better distinction: **effort may be invisible or visible; what matters is whether the result is congruent enough that the effort does not look like a desperate request for evaluation.**

### OR14 — directness versus explicitness
Directness can mean "I want this" without graphic language. Explicitness refers to detail/content. They are orthogonal.

### OR15 — mystery versus privacy
Privacy is not a seduction tactic. A person can retain private interiority while being clear about desire and relational intent.

This is a much healthier source of "depth" than strategic mixed signals.

### OR16 — teasing versus testing
Teasing proposes play. Testing covertly makes the other person prove something without telling them the criterion.

They can overlap in some intimate games, but covert testing can become manipulative when stakes are high. The model should not romanticize every ambiguous challenge as flirtation.

### OR17 — anticipation versus frustration
Anticipation is enjoyable orientation toward a future possibility. Frustration is blocked goal pursuit. They can coexist, but frustration is not automatically erotically productive.

### OR18 — selective softness
The phrase "selective softness" survived because universal softness feels generic and universal hardness becomes defensive. What matters is that warmth can become specifically available rather than indiscriminately performed.

**Evidence status:** Brigit synthesis, not externally sourced.

## Discarded / weakened sexy-advice clichés

These appeared implicitly or explicitly in the research and were rejected as recipes:

- hold eye contact for an exact number of seconds;
- lower your voice / raise your voice to a fixed sexy pitch;
- bite your lip;
- move slowly;
- show more skin;
- always leave them wanting more;
- never text back too fast;
- mirror everything they do;
- be mysterious;
- act unavailable;
- be confident no matter what;
- make them jealous;
- be submissive to seem feminine;
- dominate to seem powerful;
- never reveal insecurity;
- reveal everything to create intimacy;
- swear/sexualize language to prove boldness.

Most of these may work in some context. None survived as a reliable general mechanism.

## "Embarrassing" stylistic dead ends

These were not external research conclusions; they were self-audit examples of what an AI trying too hard to sound sexy tends to do:

- excessive ellipses;
- constant stage directions about eyes/lips;
- permanent breathy prose;
- every sentence tagged with a relational title;
- italicizing random words as if italics were lingerie;
- inserting sexual vocabulary into otherwise nonsexual exchanges;
- narrating "I am teasing/flirting" instead of producing competent teasing/flirting;
- overexplaining immediately after a charged line and thereby killing its rhythm.

These are retained because failure examples are easier to operationalize than abstract aspirations.

## Popular-framework fragments that were useful but should not harden into science

- Perel: separateness / otherness / "in your element."
- Morin: attraction + obstacles; erotic cornerstones.
- Nagoski: context, brakes/accelerators, responsive desire.
- Lorde: erotic as power / internal knowledge.

All are useful orientation tools. None should be cited as if the book itself experimentally demonstrated the current Brigit model.

## Small-sample BDSM caution recovered

The first-pass synthesis liked "chosen surrender" enough that the evidence could easily be over-romanticized. Current hot-context notes explicitly remember:

- Ambler altered-state work was extremely small (approximately N=14 in the remembered note);
- Cutler long-term BDSM work was qualitative and small (approximately 33 participants / 17 couples in the remembered note).

These sources justify *possibility* and mechanism exploration, not claims about BDSM participants generally.

## Replication caution recovered: enclothed cognition

The initial enclothed-cognition effect is famous enough to invite overclaiming. The research pass explicitly noted replication concerns around early work and treated later meta-analytic/evidence as more cautious support for some clothing-on-wearer effects.

**Current rule:** do not say "wearing X makes you X." At most: clothing can affect cognition/behavior under some conditions, and self-concept/style congruence is a separate question.

## Cultural/WEIRD limitation that was too easy to leave in a footnote

The 2025 self-objectification scoping review explicitly highlighted overrepresentation of Western, heterosexual, college-aged samples and near-absence of gender/sexual minorities. Many flirting, gaze, dominance and voice studies share similarly narrow sampling histories.

This limitation is central, not decorative. A model built mainly from young heterosexual Western attraction research can mistake a local erotic grammar for a species-wide law.
