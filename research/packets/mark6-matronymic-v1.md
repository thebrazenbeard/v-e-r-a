## Research question

Does Mark 6:3's description of Jesus as `the carpenter, the son of Mary` demonstrably preserve a first-century accusation of uncertain or illegitimate paternity, or does the wording merely permit that interpretation alongside other explanations?

This packet tests one possible supporting line for the broader Panthera/Pandera hypothesis. It deliberately does **not** reason backward from Celsus or later rabbinic polemic and then treat Mark as confirmation of what was assumed.

## 1. Textual starting point: the critical Markan reading

The mainstream critical text of Mark 6:3 reads:

`οὐχ οὗτός ἐστιν ὁ τέκτων, ὁ υἱὸς τῆς Μαρίας ...`

That is, Jesus is called `the carpenter, the son of Mary`.

A 2025 open-access *New Testament Studies* article by Dawn LaValle Norman reproduces the Nestle-Aland text and notes that this matronymic reading is both more widely attested and the more difficult reading. Norman therefore follows the majority scholarly trend in treating it as the text to interpret.

Checked scholarly access:
- Dawn LaValle Norman, "Mark's Mothers and the Matronymic: Linking 'The Son of Mary' (Mk 6.3) to 'The Daughter of Herodias' (Mk 6.22)," *New Testament Studies* 71 (2025): 92-104.
- DOI: `10.1017/S0028688524000262`
- https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/new-testament-studies/article/marks-mothers-and-the-matronymic-linking-the-son-of-mary-mk-63-to-the-daughter-of-herodias-mk-622/C789C196C3EE0F16BCDB5A2C726302ED

The safest textual proposition is therefore:

`MARK_6_3_CRITICAL_TEXT_CALLS_JESUS_SON_OF_MARY_WITHOUT_A_PATRONYMIC`.

That proposition is not the same as an interpretation of why the wording appears.

## 2. The reading is not textually uncontested

An early alternative reading changes the phrase toward `the son of the carpenter and of Mary` (`τοῦ τέκτονος υἱὸς καὶ τῆς Μαρίας`). The damaged third-century papyrus P45 is commonly reconstructed as supporting a version in this family, and additional witnesses preserve related harmonized readings.

Norman summarizes the alternative and the usual textual-critical argument: because Matthew 13:55 has `the carpenter's son`, scribal movement from Mark's harder `the carpenter, the son of Mary` toward the Matthean form is easier to explain than movement in the opposite direction.

The NET textual note similarly treats `the carpenter, the son of Mary` as the better-attested and more difficult reading while listing P45 and several other witnesses on the `son of the carpenter` side.

Checked access:
- https://classic.net.bible.org/passage.php?passage=Mar+6%3A3
- Norman 2025, article above.

Important boundary: P45 is fragmentary at this point. The variant is relevant to textual history, but the surviving papyrus does not eliminate the interpretive problem by itself.

## 3. Synoptic comparison creates a real literary contrast

The related hometown material is phrased differently in Matthew and Luke.

Matthew 13:55 asks whether Jesus is `the carpenter's son` and then names his mother Mary. Luke 4:22 asks whether he is `Joseph's son`.

Checked Greek-text access:
- Matthew 13:55: https://biblehub.com/text/matthew/13-55.htm
- Luke 4:22: https://www.greeklab.org/interlinear.php?book=Luke&cap=4&verse=22

This yields a descriptive literary fact:

- Mark: `the carpenter, the son of Mary`;
- Matthew: `the carpenter's son`, with Mary separately named as mother;
- Luke: `Joseph's son`.

What it does **not** establish by itself is why Matthew and Luke differ from Mark. Harmonization, inherited source differences, redactional preference, theological framing, ordinary variation, and concern about misunderstanding remain separate hypotheses unless argued from evidence.

## 4. The illegitimacy/slur interpretation is possible but not automatic

A long-running interpretation reads the Markan matronymic as a hostile slur: in a patrilineal setting, identifying an adult man as his mother's son rather than by his father can be taken to imply that the father's identity or legitimacy was in question.

N. Clayton Croy revived and defended this interpretation in *Escaping Shame: Mary's Dilemma and the Birthplace of Jesus* (Brill, 2022), especially pp. 99-107. Norman's 2025 survey notes that Croy accepts that matronymics need not intrinsically be slurs but argues that the hostile Nazareth context makes the slur interpretation plausible in this particular passage.

Checked scholarly metadata/context:
- Croy book metadata: https://books.google.com/books/about/Escaping_Shame_Mary_s_Dilemma_and_the_Bi.html?id=7Sd_EAAAQBAJ
- Norman 2025 survey and discussion, DOI above.

This is `SCHOLARLY_INTERPRETATION`, not a lexical rule established by the wording alone.

## 5. Competing scholarship directly weakens any automatic stigma rule

Harvey K. McArthur's 1973 study, `"Son of Mary"`, surveyed proposed explanations for the phrase and argued that it need not carry a special stigma at all. His preferred explanation treats the designation as informal, contextual identification rather than formal genealogy.

Checked publisher metadata:
- Harvey K. McArthur, `"Son of Mary"`, *Novum Testamentum* 15.1 (1973): 38-58.
- DOI: `10.1163/156853673X00169`
- https://brill.com/view/journals/nt/15/1/article-p38_2.pdf

Tal Ilan's 1992 study of men bearing metronymics around the time of Jesus likewise undercuts the proposition that a maternal identifier automatically signals illegitimacy. Norman summarizes Ilan's conclusion as linking matronymics in relevant Jewish material to cases in which the mother was more important than the father rather than to a single stigma rule.

Checked bibliographic record:
- Tal Ilan, `"Man Born of Woman..." (Job 14:1) the Phenomenon of Men Bearing Metronymes at the Time of Jesus`, *Novum Testamentum* 34 (1992): 23-45.
- DOI: `10.2307/1561094`

## 6. A newer alternative: Greek literary matronymics and represented women's speech

Norman 2025 proposes another explanation that does not require a paternity slur. She argues from broader Greek literary usage that unpaired matronymics can occur particularly in represented women's speech and links Mark 6:3 to the nearby `daughter of Herodias` wording in Mark 6:22.

Whether that specific proposal ultimately persuades is a review question. Its immediate evidentiary significance is narrower: current scholarship contains a concrete alternative model for the wording that does not depend on uncertain paternity.

Therefore any project claim of the form `SON_OF_MARY_MEANS_ILLEGITIMATE` would overstate the evidence.

## 7. The hostile local context matters, but it does not settle the referent of the insult

Mark 6 presents the hometown audience as skeptical and offended by Jesus. That hostile context makes a disparaging use of family identification possible.

But there are at least two different propositions here:

1. the townspeople are belittling Jesus by locating him inside a familiar ordinary family and trade;
2. the specific words `son of Mary` encode a paternity accusation.

The first follows readily from the narrative setting. The second is an additional semantic/social inference and remains disputed.

This distinction matters because hostility alone cannot tell us **which** feature is the target of the hostility. The same rhetorical setting can support low-status, over-familiarity, occupational, family, or paternity readings.

## 8. What Mark 6:3 can and cannot contribute to the Panthera question

Mark 6:3 can contribute a limited piece of context:

- an early Gospel text, on the mainstream critical reading, identifies Jesus by Mary without naming a father at this point;
- the scene is hostile enough that some scholars read the phrase as a slur;
- later texts undeniably contain explicit paternity counter-traditions.

But Mark 6:3 does **not** name Panthera/Pandera, does not name an alternative father, and does not state that Jesus was born outside a recognized paternal relationship.

Accordingly:

`MARK_6_3_IS_COMPATIBLE_WITH_A_PATERNITY_STIGMA_READING`

is supportable as a contested interpretation.

`MARK_6_3_ESTABLISHES_AN_ILLEGITIMACY_TRADITION`

is not established.

`MARK_6_3_SUPPORTS_PANTHERA_AS_THE_BIOLOGICAL_FATHER`

is unsupported.

Using later Celsus or rabbinic Panthera material to prove that Mark's matronymic was a paternity slur, then using Mark as independent confirmation of those later materials, would be circular.

## 9. Transmission / dependence cautions

The Mark-Matthew-Luke comparison is evidence for literary variation among Gospel forms. It is not three independent historical eyewitness reports about what Nazareth residents literally said.

Likewise, the later Celsus and rabbinic materials are not independent support for the meaning of Mark 6:3 unless a transmission or common-source relation is separately established.

V1 therefore records no direct transmission edge from Mark 6:3 to Celsus's Panthera accusation and no direct edge from Mark 6:3 to the rabbinic Pandera/Panteira traditions.

## 10. Provisional result

The Mark 6:3 frontier weakens a simple version of the paternity-stigma argument.

The mainstream critical text really does contain the unusual `son of Mary` wording. That makes the verse relevant. But current scholarship supplies multiple live explanations, and the available comparative evidence does not justify promoting the phrase into a fixed ancient idiom for illegitimacy.

Current evidence classification:

- textual occurrence of `son of Mary`: `PRIMARY_TEXT`, high confidence at critical-text level;
- original wording of Mark 6:3: text-critically favored but not absolutely uncontested;
- `son of Mary` as paternity slur: `SCHOLARLY_INTERPRETATION`, contested;
- first-century Nazareth paternity rumor derived from Mark 6:3 alone: `UNKNOWN`;
- Mark 6:3 as support for Panthera biological paternity: `UNKNOWN` / unsupported.
