## Research question

Is `Panthera/Pantera/Pandera` demonstrably derived from Greek `παρθένος` (`parthenos`, virgin/maiden) as an anti-Christian pun, or is that one plausible polemical explanation among several?

The distinction matters because the wordplay hypothesis can be used in opposite ways:

- to argue that Panthera is merely a joke generated from the virgin-birth claim and therefore carries no independent biographical information;
- or, if treated carelessly, to claim a proven linguistic lineage that the evidence does not actually establish.

V1 tests the derivation itself rather than assuming either conclusion.

## 1. Primary textual anchor: Panthera is actually in Origen's report of Celsus

Origen, *Contra Celsum* I.32, reports the named figure `Panthera` in Celsus's paternity counter-story. That textual occurrence is independent of any explanation for how the name entered the story.

Checked primary access:
- Origen, *Contra Celsum* I.32: https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04161.htm

Therefore:

`Celsus/Origen contains Panthera` = textual occurrence.

`Panthera was coined from parthenos` = separate linguistic/source-provenance hypothesis.

## 2. Boyarin gives a clear positive formulation of the wordplay model

Daniel Boyarin (1998) explicitly revisits the old Cassel/Patterson proposal. He contrasts Christian `ἐκ παρθένου` (from a virgin) with a taunting `ἐκ πανθήρος` / Panthera formulation and argues that Panthera is an intentional distortion of `parthenos`, a form of polemical `cacophemism`.

Boyarin also makes an important point against a simplistic objection: the fact that `Panthera` was a genuinely attested personal name does not logically rule out its being selected or exploited as a pun. A real name can be recruited into wordplay.

Checked full-text scholarly access:
- Daniel Boyarin, `Martyrdom and the Making of Christianity and Judaism`, *Journal of Early Christian Studies* 6.4 (1998), pp. 577-627, especially pp. 599-600 / note 69.
- DOI: 10.1353/earl.1998.0053
- author-uploaded full text surfaced through ResearchGate.

Boyarin's proposition is `SCHOLARLY_INTERPRETATION`, not a recovered ancient explanation of the name.

## 3. Blumell treats the wordplay as possible, not settled

Lincoln Blumell (2007), while studying the Jewish material in Celsus, notes that it is uncertain whether `Panthera` is a play on `parthenos` from the virgin-birth tradition or a reference to an actual figure. He also records another historical proposal: a sexual/panther association intended to imply promiscuity.

Checked publisher article:
- Lincoln Blumell, `A Jew in Celsus' True Doctrine? An examination of Jewish Anti-Christian polemic in the second century C.E.`, 2007.
- DOI: 10.1177/000842980703600206

This is important because it prevents the project from turning a widely repeated explanation into settled etymology.

## 4. Jaffé supports polemical/mocking function, but function is not identical to derivation

Dan Jaffé's philological/historical study of `Ben Pantera` treats the designation as an old polemical expression of mockery toward Jesus within Jewish-Christian rivalry.

Checked scholarly discovery/record:
- Dan Jaffé, `Une ancienne dénomination talmudique de Jésus : Ben Pantera : essai d'analyse philologique et historique`, *Theologische Zeitschrift* 64 (2008), 258-270.
- repository DOI: 10.5169/seals-877834
- SciSpace record/abstract confirms the article's focus on the expression as mockery/scorn in Jewish-Christian polemic.

V1 separates two propositions:

- `Ben Pantera functions polemically`: supported as scholarly interpretation;
- `Ben Pantera is specifically generated by mechanical transformation of parthenos`: requires additional argument and is not established merely by the polemical function.

## 5. Murcia demonstrates that the origin question remains genuinely open

Thierry Murcia's 2014 article is explicitly a `status quaestionis` on the origin of the Panthera name and investigates multiple hypotheses. The journal record confirms that the name's origin is itself a research problem rather than a closed lexical fact.

## 6. The forms are similar enough for deliberate wordplay, but not a regular etymology demonstrated by sound law

The relevant Greek forms are visibly similar but not identical:

- `παρθένος` — parthenos
- `Πανθήρα / Panthera` — Panthera/Panthera name-form in the tradition

The proposed relation involves rearrangement/substitution rather than a demonstrated regular historical phonological development. Boyarin himself frames it as intentional distortion/taunt, which is the appropriate linguistic category if the hypothesis is correct.

Therefore V1 rejects the stronger statement:

`PANTHERA_IS_ETYMologically_DERIVED_FROM_PARTHENOS_BY_REGULAR_SOUND_CHANGE`.

The live proposition is instead:

`PANTHERA_MAY_HAVE_BEEN_SELECTED_OR_DISTORTED_AS_A_POLEMICAL_ECHO_OF_PARTHENOS`.

That is a wordplay/source-choice hypothesis.

## 7. Real-name attestation cuts both ways

The Tiberius Julius Abdes Pantera inscription demonstrates that Pantera/Panthera was a real personal name in the Roman imperial world. Boyarin argues this can actually make a pun more effective because the result sounds like a genuine name.

But the same fact also removes any need to derive every occurrence of Panthera from `parthenos`: a real name can enter a story as a real name, as a selected pun, as a coincidental resemblance, or through another tradition.

Thus:

`name attested` does not prove wordplay;

and

`name attested` does not disprove wordplay.

It simply blocks the false dichotomy.

## 8. Chronology does not solve the derivation by itself

Celsus's late-second-century accusation is early enough to show that Panthera was already attached to the counter-story by that stage. Later rabbinic `Pandera/Panteira` forms show continued or parallel use.

Neither chronology alone tells us whether:

- Celsus inherited a Jewish pun already based on Christian `parthenos` language;
- Celsus or another intermediary generated/reframed the name;
- the rabbinic forms derive from the Celsus-era tradition;
- both draw on another source;
- or a real personal/name tradition was later polemically reinterpreted.

Those are transmission claims, not consequences of spelling similarity.

## 9. Current evidence classification

- Panthera in Origen's report of Celsus: `PRIMARY_TEXT`, high confidence.
- Pantera/Panthera as an independently attested Roman personal name: `MATERIAL_EVIDENCE`, high confidence at name/person-attestation level.
- deliberate `parthenos -> Panthera` polemical distortion: `SCHOLARLY_INTERPRETATION`, plausible and supported by Boyarin/Cassel/Patterson tradition, but contested/not demonstrated.
- polemical function of `Ben Pantera`: `SCHOLARLY_INTERPRETATION`, supported by Jaffé and related scholarship.
- regular etymological derivation by sound change: `UNKNOWN` / unsupported.
- Panthera wordplay proves there was no historical person behind the name: `UNKNOWN` / unsupported.
- real-name attestation proves the Celsus figure was a historical biological father: `UNKNOWN` / unsupported.

## 10. Consequence for the paternity hypothesis

The wordplay hypothesis cannot responsibly be used as a magic eraser of the Panthera tradition, but neither can the existence of real people named Pantera turn the tradition into biography.

The strongest current conclusion is:

`PANTHERA/PANDERA_WORDPLAY_WITH_PARTHENOS_IS_A_PLAUSIBLE_POLEMICAL_MODEL, NOT_AN_ESTABLISHED_ETYMOLOGY_OR_TRANSMISSION_FACT`.

That keeps both live alternatives visible without letting either become hidden evidence for or against biological paternity.
