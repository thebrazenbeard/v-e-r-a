## Purpose

Simulation theory contains at least three different philosophical questions:

1. **Provenance:** Is our world implemented by an external substrate?
2. **Reality:** If it is, are the objects and events we experience nevertheless real?
3. **Knowledge:** Could embedded observers know whether they are simulated?

Those questions can receive different answers.

## 1. Simulation skepticism and brain-in-a-vat structure

A sufficiently immersive simulation can reproduce the classical skeptical structure:

- an observer has experiences that appear to be caused by ordinary external objects;
- a hidden mechanism generates an indistinguishable experience stream;
- the observer asks whether current evidence discriminates the ordinary and skeptical scenarios.

This resembles brain-in-a-vat, dream, evil-demon, and other external-world skeptical arguments.

But epistemic possibility is not probability.

Project guard:

`I cannot rule out H from current experience` != `H is probable`.

## 2. Chalmers: simulation realism

David Chalmers argues in *Reality+* and the 2024 Oxford debate for three broad theses:

1. **Metaphysics:** if we are in a full simulation, objects around us can still be real objects rather than mere illusions.
2. **Epistemology:** we cannot know with certainty that we are not in a simulation.
3. **Value:** a life in virtual reality can still be meaningful/good.

Relevant sources:
- David J. Chalmers, `Précis of Reality+` (2024).
- David J. Chalmers, `The Simulation Hypothesis: Metaphysics, Epistemology, Value` (2024).
- https://academic.oup.com/book/58199/chapter-abstract/481535740
- https://academic.oup.com/book/58199/chapter-abstract/481536050

### Structural realism intuition

The key move is to treat relations implemented in a virtual environment as genuine relations. A simulated table can be real as a virtual/simulated table if stable causal/structural relations support interaction with it.

Under this view, discovering simulation would revise the metaphysical nature of our environment rather than proving that everything we believed about tables, people, or cities was simply false.

## 3. Horgan: phenomenal intentionality objection

Terry Horgan challenges Chalmers's realism.

Source:
- Terry Horgan, `Why Virtual Worlds Aren't Real: How Phenomenal Intentionality Constrains Mental Reference` (2024).
- https://academic.oup.com/book/58199/chapter-abstract/481535867

Horgan argues that phenomenal intentionality places constraints on what simulated agents' beliefs can successfully refer to. On his account, many simulated agents' apparent environment-directed beliefs fail to secure the references needed for Chalmers's realism.

Project posture:

This is a direct metaphysical disagreement, not a question settled by the simulation hypothesis itself.

## 4. Peacocke: metaphysics and epistemology objection

Christopher Peacocke disputes both the claim that spatial virtual objects are straightforwardly real and the claim that we cannot know we are not simulated.

Source:
- Christopher Peacocke, `Simulation: Its Metaphysics and Epistemology` (2024).
- https://academic.oup.com/book/58199/chapter-abstract/481535964

V1 currently has abstract-level access to the chapter and therefore records only the published dispute scope, not a detailed reconstruction of arguments unavailable in current readback.

## 5. Realism is not provenance evidence

Suppose Chalmers is correct that virtual objects can be real.

That does not increase the probability that our world is simulated by itself.

It answers a conditional question:

`If simulated, what would still count as real?`

Likewise, Horgan or Peacocke succeeding against simulation realism would not necessarily prove base reality. It could instead imply that simulated agents are systematically mistaken about object reference.

## 6. Implementation artifact versus provenance

Even if an embedded observer discovers:

- finite resolution;
- a lattice;
- computational update rules;
- information bounds;
- a hidden error-correction structure;

there remains a separate inference from `this world has implementation-like structure` to `this structure is implemented by an external simulator`.

A base-level digital ontology could reproduce some of the same evidence.

Therefore:

`detectable implementation-like structure` != `demonstrated external provenance`.

## 7. Underdetermination

A sufficiently unconstrained simulation hypothesis can mimic any observation because the simulator can simply be stipulated to produce it.

Examples:

- no artifacts -> simulator is perfect;
- artifacts -> simulator leaked artifacts;
- apparent randomness -> simulator uses randomness;
- apparent determinism -> simulator is deterministic;
- miracle-like event -> simulator intervened;
- no interventions -> simulator is non-interventionist.

This flexibility creates an underdetermination problem.

Project principle:

An explanatory model that can accommodate every possible observation without differential predictions gains little empirical support from any one observation.

## 8. Bayesian discrimination requires likelihood differences

To treat evidence E as favoring simulation S over alternative A, one needs some reason that:

`P(E | S) > P(E | A)`.

If simulation is left unspecified, `P(E | S)` is poorly defined.

For example:

- Fine-tuning could be expected under some purposeful simulations but not under arbitrary simulations.
- Lattice artifacts could be expected under lattice simulations but not analog simulations.
- Low computational complexity might be expected under resource-minimizing simulators but not under simulators with vast resources.

The generic hypothesis cannot simply inherit predictions from whichever narrow submodel fits the data after observation.

## 9. Simulator communication/intervention

A simulator could hypothetically reveal itself through:

- stable messages encoded in otherwise unexplained physical phenomena;
- repeatable controlled violations of ordinary laws;
- direct interface/contact;
- implementation metadata accessible from within the world.

But even an apparently intentional message would raise authentication problems:

- an in-world intelligence could fake it;
- an unknown physical process could mimic part of it;
- a sufficiently advanced alien could appear godlike;
- a simulator identity claim could be false.

The stronger the intervention and independent verification, the stronger the provenance evidence, but `extraordinary agent` and `external simulator` remain separate identifications.

## 10. Nested simulation skepticism

If a simulator can itself be simulated, discovering one outer layer would not establish base reality.

This creates a regress/termination problem:

- finite nesting;
- infinite nesting;
- cycles/self-simulation;
- base level;
- no privileged base level.

The possibility of nesting weakens the inference from `we found a simulator` to `we found ultimate reality`.

## 11. Meaning and value

Simulation discovery does not automatically imply nihilism.

Chalmers argues that virtual/simulated lives can contain real relationships, agency, knowledge, and value.

Grace Helton's 2024 response complicates the picture by emphasizing the importance of social knowledge for meaningful life.

Project point:

The value question depends strongly on whether other minds are real/conscious, whether relationships are authentic, and whether the simulator manipulates memories/personhood. It is not settled by ontology alone.

## 12. Epistemic levels

V1 distinguishes:

### Level A: logical/metaphysical possibility

`A simulation scenario is coherent.`

This is weak evidence about actuality.

### Level B: physical possibility

`A simulation architecture is consistent with known physical/computational constraints.`

Still not actuality evidence.

### Level C: explanatory fit

`A simulation submodel makes observed features expected.`

Needs comparison with alternatives.

### Level D: discriminatory empirical evidence

`Observed E is substantially more expected under a specified simulation model than serious alternatives.`

Potential evidence.

### Level E: provenance evidence

`Evidence directly identifies an external implementing process/agent.`

Strongest category, presently absent for our universe.

## 14. Hostile questions

1. Is the generic simulation hypothesis distinguishable from skeptical scenarios by any observation?
2. If a model is made testable by specifying implementation details, are we testing simulation or merely those details?
3. Can an embedded observer ever prove external implementation rather than fundamental digital ontology?
4. Does simulation realism require causal relations that cross the substrate/world boundary?
5. What would count as authentic simulator communication?
6. Does nested simulation make `base reality` empirically inaccessible even after a simulator is found?
7. Could a world be computationally implemented with no intentional simulator at all?
