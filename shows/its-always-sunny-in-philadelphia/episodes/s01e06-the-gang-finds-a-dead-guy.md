## Synopsis and beat map
The Gang discovers an elderly patron dead in a Paddy's booth and focuses first on the sanitation and reputational inconvenience. His granddaughter Rebecca arrives seeking information about him; Mac and Dennis instantly fabricate intimate friendships with the dead man in competing attempts to sleep with her. Dee, disturbed by the abandonment implied by his lonely death, decides to visit her own grandfather Pop-Pop in a nursing home and recruits Charlie because she fears old people. Dee panics and leaves Charlie alone; Pop-Pop mistakes Charlie for Dennis and asks him to recover a box of wartime belongings. Mac and Dennis recruit homeless men to pose as the deceased man's friends at his memorial. Charlie and Mac discover Pop-Pop's box contains Nazi memorabilia and a uniform; attempts to place it with a museum fail, and they burn the material. Dennis later brags that he slept with Rebecca just before learning the implication of Pop-Pop's history.

## Character state
- **Dennis / Mac:** competitive sexual opportunism overwhelms even a funeral. Dennis weaponizes social performance and public speaking; Mac is increasingly frustrated by Dennis's belief that he will always win these competitions.
- **Dee:** her moral concern is genuine enough to trigger the Pop-Pop visit, but her fear of aging and bodily decline sends her fleeing from the actual encounter.
- **Charlie:** handles the nursing home more directly than Dee and becomes the accidental recipient of Reynolds-family history because Pop-Pop mistakes him for Dennis.

## Themes / comic mechanism
Mortality produces almost no introspection. Death is converted into sexual opportunity, image management, and a bizarre family-history errand. The episode also complicates the idea that family connection is inherently redemptive: the estranged relative Dee tries to reconnect with turns out to carry virulent antisemitism and Nazi history.

## Continuity / later relevance
- First major Pop-Pop story and foundational Reynolds-family history.
- Shows Mac/Dennis sexual competition as an explicit internal hierarchy.
- Reinforces Charlie as someone the Gang often sends into unpleasant practical situations because he tolerates them better.
