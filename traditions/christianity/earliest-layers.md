# Earliest surviving Christian layers

The chronological order of surviving texts matters because later readers often project mature doctrine backward into earlier sources.

## Paul — roughly 50s CE

Paul's undisputed letters are earlier than the canonical Gospels. They presuppose Yeshua's death, resurrection belief, messianic significance, communities of followers, and developing interpretations of his status.

They do **not** provide a narrative infancy story comparable to Matthew or Luke. This absence is not proof against miraculous conception; it is evidence that the earliest surviving Christian corpus does not foreground that story.

Paul already contains very elevated claims about Christ, so the history is not a simple staircase from "mere human" to "divine". Different kinds of high Christology can appear early while still developing in form and vocabulary.

## Mark — commonly dated around 65–70 CE

Mark is widely treated as the earliest surviving canonical Gospel. It begins with adult Yeshua/Jesus and John the Baptist rather than with a birth narrative.

Important features for this project:

- Kingdom proclamation is central.
- Jesus is embedded in Jewish scripture and controversy.
- A hometown rejection scene calls him "son of Mary."
- The narrative moves toward Jerusalem, Temple conflict, Roman crucifixion, and resurrection proclamation.
- It does not narrate a virgin conception.

## Matthew and Luke — commonly dated after Mark

Both contain infancy narratives and miraculous conception, but they are not one interchangeable story.

Matthew foregrounds Joseph, dreams, Magi, Herod, danger, Egypt, and scriptural fulfillment.

Luke foregrounds Mary, annunciation, Elizabeth, hymnic material, shepherds, Temple presentation, and the age-twelve Temple episode.

Both connect Yeshua to Davidic/messianic expectation and Bethlehem, but their narrative routes and emphases differ significantly.

Do not create a synthetic Christmas story and then mistake the synthesis for either source.

## John — commonly dated late first century

John does not retell Matthew/Luke-style infancy material. Instead, its prologue identifies the Logos/Word as pre-existent, involved in creation, and becoming flesh.

This is a different explanatory register:

- Matthew/Luke: extraordinary conception and messianic birth.
- John: cosmic pre-existence and incarnation.

Later Christian doctrine combines them, but source analysis should preserve the distinction.

## Why chronology matters

The following propositions have different histories:

- Yeshua was Messiah.
- Yeshua was raised from death.
- Yeshua was uniquely God's Son.
- Yeshua existed before earthly birth.
- Yeshua participated in creation.
- Yeshua was conceived without a human father.
- Yeshua is ontologically one with God in a later Trinitarian formulation.

Christian theology eventually relates these claims into one system. Historical study asks when and how each form becomes visible.
