# Early Buddhism — Diachronic Overview V1

Status: RESEARCH_PACKET / REVIEW_REQUIRED

## Purpose

This packet reconstructs early Buddhism as a historically developing Indian religious movement before later Theravada, Mahayana, Vajrayana, or modern philosophical syntheses are projected backward onto the earliest recoverable layers.

## 1. Source-history problem

The earliest Buddhist discourses survive in school-specific textual collections rather than stenographic first-generation records. Pali Nikayas, Chinese Agamas, Sanskrit and other witnesses preserve overlapping but not identical material.

Roderick Bucknell's 2022 reconstruction makes those discrepancies part of the evidence: comparative analysis across early school traditions can sometimes identify older shared structures, but reconstruction remains historical inference rather than direct access to Gautama's exact speech.

V1 therefore distinguishes:
- attributed-event or teaching setting;
- oral transmission history;
- collection/redaction history;
- physical witness;
- modern edition/translation.

## 2. Suffering, cessation, and path

SN 56.11 presents the familiar fourfold structure of suffering, origin/craving, cessation, and path, with the Noble Eightfold Path as the practice leading toward cessation.

The project treats this as a primary textual configuration of an early Buddhist tradition. Whether every formulation or ordering goes back verbatim to the historical Buddha is a separate historical-reconstruction question.

## 3. Not-self

SN 22.59 applies not-self analysis to form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, and consciousness.

The claim is not safely translated as a simple Western proposition such as 'humans do not exist.' Its function is tied to non-identification, impermanence/affliction, liberation from clinging, and analysis of the aggregates.

Comparison guard: Buddhist anatta/anatman must not be collapsed into materialism, nihilism, or a hidden Upanishadic atman without source-specific argument.

## 4. Karma and rebirth

Early Buddhism operates inside an Indian environment where karma and rebirth had become major religious problems, but it reworks them.

Unlike the Rigveda, where mature karmic rebirth is absent in the reconstruction already registered in PR #21, early Buddhist discourse clearly presupposes repeated birth and karmically consequential action.

Yet Buddhism rejects the need for an unchanging self as the carrier of rebirth. This produces one of the tradition's central conceptual tensions for later interpreters: continuity without a permanent personal essence.

## 5. Dependent origination

SN 12 traditions present dependent origination as conditional arising rather than creation by a first cause. The chain involving ignorance, formations, consciousness, name-and-form, sense bases, contact, feeling, craving, clinging, becoming, birth, aging and death is one major formulation.

Later exegetical systems interpret these links in different temporal and psychological ways. V1 does not promote one later interpretation as the original meaning.

## 6. Nirvana

Nirvana/nibbana is the cessation goal associated with extinguishing craving, liberation, and ending the cycle of suffering/rebirth.

V1 does not force it into either 'annihilation' or 'eternal metaphysical existence.' Early Buddhist texts and later schools frame the goal in multiple registers, and modern scholarship remains contested over how much positive metaphysics should be inferred.

## 7. Meditation and liberation practice

Early Buddhist practice includes ethical discipline, concentration/meditation, mindfulness, insight, restraint, and monastic training.

Bucknell emphasizes reconstructing the path through comparison of parallel early textual traditions. His conclusions remain scholarly reconstruction, not repository fact.

## 8. Sangha and social form

Early Buddhism becomes not merely a teaching but an organized mendicant community. Monastic discipline, teacher-student transmission, patronage, lay support, ordination, and communal recitation shape the preservation and institutional development of the tradition.

The sangha must not be treated as socially isolated from Brahmanical, Jain, urban, mercantile, royal, and household religious worlds.

## 9. Relation to Brahmanical traditions

The old model 'Buddhism versus one monolithic Brahmanism' is too simple.

Nathan McGovern argues that early Buddhism is better located among fluid fifth-century-BCE Brahmanical and renunciant intellectual currents rather than outside a single finished orthodoxy. This is a scholarly model, not a settled lineage claim.

Shared vocabulary or practices may reflect:
- common Indian religious environment;
- polemical redefinition;
- adaptation;
- parallel development;
- direct teacher/tradition contact.

These possibilities must be tested separately.

## 10. Cosmology and gods

Early Buddhist texts retain devas, Brahma worlds, Mara, heavens, hells, rebirth realms, and large-scale cosmology. Buddhism is therefore not accurately represented as simply 'atheism' if that means denial of all divine/supernatural beings.

At the same time, liberation does not depend on a creator God, and gods themselves remain within conditioned existence in many Buddhist frameworks.

## 11. Later development guard

Do not silently project backward:
- classical Theravada Abhidhamma;
- Mahayana bodhisattva systems;
- Madhyamaka or Yogacara philosophy;
- Buddha-nature doctrine;
- tantric/Vajrayana systems;
- modern secular mindfulness;
- modern 'Buddhism is just psychology' claims.

These are later or distinct developmental layers.

## 12. Current project model

The strongest V1 reconstruction is that early Buddhism emerges from a plural late-Vedic/renunciant Indian environment, accepts karma/rebirth as a real problem-space, rejects permanent-self identification, diagnoses suffering through craving and conditioned processes, and develops a disciplined path aimed at release from that process.

This is HISTORICAL_RECONSTRUCTION. It does not claim that one surviving canon perfectly reproduces the historical Buddha's exact teaching.
