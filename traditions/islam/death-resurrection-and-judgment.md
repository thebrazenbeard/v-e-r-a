# Death, resurrection, and judgment

## Re-creation

The Qur'an repeatedly argues that the creator who originated life can create again. Resurrection is therefore framed not as an impossible reversal but as a second act of the same sovereign creative power.

Natural cycles — especially dead earth becoming alive with rain — are used as analogies for resurrection.

## Pattern versus current embodiment — project analogy

The Qur'anic argument can be mapped speculatively as:

1. original instantiation demonstrates creator capability;
2. present biological state terminates;
3. termination does not limit root authority;
4. the agent can be instantiated again for judgment/life to come.

This resembles **re-instantiation from authoritative identity/state** more than "a ghost simply floats away forever."

The Qur'an itself does not describe backups, data structures, or digital identity.

## Judgment

Resurrection is tied to moral accountability. Humans are not merely restarted; deeds, intentions, belief, injustice, mercy and responsibility matter.

For the systems model, `evaluation` is a better analogy than `score` because Qur'anic judgment is ethically and relationally dense rather than a mechanical points system.

## Records and testimony

Qur'anic judgment imagery includes records/books, witnesses, bodily testimony, scales, and disclosure of hidden deeds. These symbols are especially fertile for modern information-system comparison but must remain in their own semantic setting first.

Possible structural mappings:

- record/book -> persistent account;
- witness -> independent evidentiary channel;
- disclosure -> privileged audit;
- scale -> evaluation/justice metaphor;
- resurrection -> renewed instantiation for adjudication.

These are **PROJECT_ANALOGY** entries.

## Present world versus final order

The Qur'an strongly distinguishes present worldly life from resurrection/judgment and the enduring life to come. The present world has consequence precisely because it is not ultimate.

This produces a structure shared, with major differences, across Jewish apocalypticism, Christianity and Islam:

`present order -> death/discontinuity -> divine intervention -> resurrection/judgment -> enduring order`

The traditions disagree over mechanisms, mediators, atonement, Jesus's role, and detailed eschatology. The shared high-level structure should not erase those disagreements.
