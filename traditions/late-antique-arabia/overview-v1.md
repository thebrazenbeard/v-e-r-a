# Late Antique Arabian Religious Landscape Overview V1

Status: `SOURCE-BASED OVERVIEW / REVIEW REQUIRED`

## Scope

Late Antique Arabia is reconstructed as a set of connected but regionally diverse societies rather than one religious system called `Jahiliyya`.

The Arabian Peninsula and its borderlands contained different political economies, scripts, cults, languages, tribal networks, Jewish communities, Christian communities, monotheist experiments, sanctuaries, pilgrimage practices, and imperial connections.

V1 therefore forbids treating South Arabia, the Hijaz, northern Arabia, and Roman/Sasanian frontier Arabia as religiously interchangeable.

## Epigraphy changes the baseline

South Arabian inscriptions provide evidence independent of later Islamic literary reconstruction.

Christian Robin's epigraphic synthesis tracks Himyar, Aksum, Arabia Deserta, Judaism, Najran, Abraha, and imperial conflict through inscriptions rather than relying only on later narrative sources.

URL:
- https://academic.oup.com/book/12636/chapter-abstract/162565588

This evidence is central because it shows that the religious world immediately preceding Islam already included substantial biblical/monotheistic vocabulary and institutions.

## Himyarite monotheism

Iwona Gajda notes that Himyarite rulers adopted monotheism in the later fourth century and that inscriptions increasingly address a single God using names/titles such as Rahmanan and Ilan/Ilahan.

The exact identity of that monotheism is historically debated:

- Judaism is strongly implicated in portions of Himyarite history;
- not every monotheist inscription maps neatly onto later rabbinic Judaism;
- later Christian rule also uses Rahmanan language.

URL:
- https://academic.oup.com/book/5204/chapter-abstract/147869817

The semantic rule is therefore:

`RAHMANAN` != automatically `classical Islamic al-Rahman`

and also != automatically one fixed Jewish theology across all inscriptions.

## Jewish Himyar

Epigraphic and literary evidence supports significant Jewish identification among Himyarite elites, especially in the period associated with Yusuf/Dhu Nuwas.

The kingdom's religious politics must be reconstructed from inscriptions, external Christian accounts, and later sources with source criticism.

Absence from rabbinic literature is not proof that Jewish Himyar did not exist; nor does a Jewish royal identification prove that every subject followed rabbinic norms.

## Najran

The killing/persecution of Christians at Najran in 523 became an important Christian martyr tradition and a major political event involving Himyar and Aksum.

Valentina Grasso's recent reconstruction emphasizes that literary sources can theologize the violence while political/economic networks and Red Sea imperial competition also mattered.

URL:
- https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/preislamic-arabia/shape-of-the-sixth-century-i/964CAE87B70D9BAAB2E7287FB15BE690

V1 therefore preserves both:

- genuine religious identity/conflict;
- political/economic/imperial causation.

Neither erases the other.

## Aksumite Christian rule and Abraha

Aksumite intervention brought Christian rule to South Arabia. Abraha became the dominant sixth-century ruler after the collapse of Jewish Himyarite rule.

Christian inscriptions continue using Rahmanan for the biblical God, now in explicitly Christian formulations.

Sigrid Kjaer's study traces this semantic history and warns against imagining one timeless meaning of Rahmanan.

## Sasanian phase

After Abraha and his successors, South Arabia eventually entered a Sasanian political phase before the rise of Islam.

That places Arabian communities at the intersection of:

- Byzantine/Roman interests;
- Aksumite/Ethiopian Christianity;
- Himyarite Jewish and monotheist traditions;
- Sasanian Iranian power;
- Arabian tribal networks.

Islam therefore emerges from an environment already connected to multiple Late Antique imperial/religious systems.

## Christianity in Arabia

Christian communities existed in multiple Arabian and borderland settings, but `Arabian Christianity` is not one denomination.

Potential affiliations/contact include:

- Church of the East communities in eastern/northeastern Arabia and Sasanian-connected zones;
- miaphysite networks through Syria and Aksum;
- Chalcedonian imperial Christianity in Roman-linked regions;
- local Arabic/Syriac/Greek/Ethiopic mixtures.

Each local claim requires direct evidence rather than assigning an Arabian Christian automatically to one Christology.

## Jewish communities beyond Himyar

Jewish populations and traditions existed in multiple Arabian settings according to later literary sources and some epigraphic/historical evidence.

The project will distinguish:

- epigraphically attested Jewish identity;
- later Islamic reports about Jewish tribes/communities;
- rabbinic-text evidence;
- external Christian/Byzantine accounts;
- historical reconstruction.

These evidence classes cannot be merged merely because they refer to the same region.

## Polytheism and sanctuary religion

Monotheist evidence does not justify erasing Arabian polytheism.

Older South Arabian inscriptions preserve pantheons and local gods, while later Islamic sources preserve memories of Arabian cult images, sanctuaries, pilgrimage, vows, divination, and deity names.

The chronological task is to determine where and when particular cults remained active.

South Arabia's move toward monotheist royal inscriptions does not prove that the entire peninsula had abandoned polytheism.

## Allah / Ilah / Rahman lexical problems

Divine naming requires semantic discipline.

Terms from Semitic roots for `god/God`, including Arabic `ilah/Allah`, South Arabian `Ilah(an)`, and `Rahman(an)`, can overlap with Jewish/Christian/monotheist vocabulary without being identical theological concepts in every source.

A shared divine epithet can indicate:

- shared Semitic vocabulary;
- translation across communities;
- polemical appropriation;
- continuity of a local divine title;
- shared biblical influence.

Which relation applies must be demonstrated.

## Arabia and `Late Antiquity`

The project treats the Qur'anic environment as part of Late Antiquity rather than as historically isolated from it.

That does **not** mean `the Qur'an is just borrowed Christianity/Judaism`.

It means that historically available inputs include:

- biblical narrative traditions;
- Syriac/Greek/Arabian Christianities;
- Jewish interpretation;
- Arabian cultic language;
- South Arabian monotheism;
- Iranian imperial presence;
- trade, pilgrimage, war, migration and diplomacy.

The Qur'an must still be studied as its own text and religious system.

## V1 guards

Do not infer:

- Arabia was uniformly pagan before Islam;
- Arabia was uniformly monotheist before Islam;
- Himyarite Judaism equals rabbinic Judaism in every respect;
- Rahmanan has one fixed meaning from Himyar through the Qur'an;
- Najran was caused only by theology or only by economics;
- all Arabian Christians shared one Christology;
- later Islamic descriptions of pre-Islamic religion are automatically eyewitness records;
- epigraphic silence proves absence.
