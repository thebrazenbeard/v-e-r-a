## Historical scope

`Mesopotamian religion` covers multiple languages, cities, dynasties, and periods across Sumerian, Akkadian, Babylonian, and Assyrian worlds. V1 therefore treats the corpus as historically layered rather than one fixed mythology.

This overview centers on Babylonian *Enuma Elish* as one major cosmogonic-political text, while preserving the distinction between that composition and the wider Mesopotamian mythic corpus.

## Divine plurality and political theology

Mesopotamian religion is polytheistic and strongly city-centered. Individual cities elevated patron deities while still participating in a wider pantheon.

*Enuma Elish* is especially important because it makes the supremacy of **Marduk**, patron deity of Babylon, part of cosmic history. The Metropolitan Museum describes the epic as theological legitimization of Marduk's rise over the older divine order and as a political account of Babylon's religious ascendancy.

That means Marduk's cosmic supremacy should be read at once as mythic theology and historically situated city/imperial ideology.

## Cosmogony in Enuma Elish

The epic begins before the named heaven and earth with mingled primeval waters associated with **Apsu** and **Tiamat**. Generations of gods emerge from this primordial condition.

Conflict escalates among the gods. Marduk receives supreme authority from the divine assembly to confront Tiamat. After defeating her, he uses her body to order the cosmos and proceeds to organize celestial and divine structures.

This is not straightforward `creation ex nihilo`. An older scholarly translation published by the Oriental Institute explicitly warns against reading Marduk's command over a garment as evidence for creation from nothing.

Classification:
- *Enuma Elish*: `PRIMARY_TEXT` for Babylonian literary theology;
- political interpretation of Marduk's elevation: `SCHOLARLY_INTERPRETATION` grounded in historical context;
- universal statement that all Mesopotamians believed one identical cosmogony: unsupported.

## Divine assembly and kingship

Marduk's authority is conferred and recognized by other gods before his battle with Tiamat. Divine kingship therefore operates within an assembly rather than as isolated omnipotence from the start of the story.

This matters comparatively because later Near Eastern texts can also imagine divine assemblies, but similar council structures must not automatically be treated as direct borrowing.

## Chaos combat

Marduk's conflict with Tiamat is a major ancient Near Eastern example of a divine warrior defeating a watery/chaotic adversary and establishing ordered rule.

This creates a legitimate comparison field with:

- Ugaritic Baal versus Yamm;
- biblical YHWH/Sea/Leviathan/Rahab combat imagery;
- later divine-war motifs.

But `combat against watery chaos` is a structural category. Specific transmission claims require chronology, contact, and textual pathway evidence.

## Humanity

In *Enuma Elish*, human creation occurs after the divine war and is tied to the reorganization of divine labor and service. Humanity's place is therefore embedded in a cosmic-social order involving gods, temples, cities, and ritual obligations.

A broader Mesopotamian anthropology packet will need to compare this with other creation traditions such as *Atrahasis*, where human creation, divine labor, population, mortality, and flood traditions are configured differently.

## Death and afterlife

Mesopotamian afterlife traditions generally differ sharply from later resurrection-centered systems. The underworld is usually imagined as a real but diminished postmortem domain, though exact representations vary by text and period.

V1 does not collapse *Gilgamesh*, *Ishtar's Descent*, funerary ritual, ghost traditions, and theological texts into one uniform doctrine. These require separate source packets.

## Comparison guard: Genesis and the Levant

Mesopotamian and biblical creation traditions share some broad ancient Near Eastern conceptual material: primeval waters, ordered cosmic domains, divine speech/command, celestial ordering, and humanity placed into a divinely structured world.

Those parallels justify comparative analysis but not a one-line claim that Genesis is simply copied from *Enuma Elish*.

The project should distinguish:

- shared ancient Near Eastern cosmological vocabulary;
- possible literary dependence;
- deliberate theological inversion or rejection;
- independent use of common environmental imagery;
- later scholarly harmonization.
