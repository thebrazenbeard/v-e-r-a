# Qur'anic Religious System Overview V1

Status: `PRIMARY-TEXT-CENTERED OVERVIEW / REVIEW REQUIRED`

## Scope

This packet reconstructs the Qur'an as its own religious/textual system before asking where particular elements came from.

The source boundary is strict:

- `QUR'ANIC TEXT` = primary evidence for what the Qur'an says;
- sira, hadith, tafsir, legal schools, kalam, later creed and medieval biography are later evidence layers;
- traditional Meccan/Medinan assignments and modern inner-Qur'anic chronologies are historical reconstructions, not verse-level self-dates;
- resemblance to Jewish, Christian, Iranian or Arabian material is not yet a transmission claim.

Angelika Neuwirth emphasizes that the Qur'an differs structurally from continuous biblical/gospel narrative: its suras form discrete rhetorical units, with early proclamation strongly oriented toward imminent judgment and later material increasingly engaged in communal/scriptural debate.

Nicolai Sinai likewise treats inner-Qur'anic chronology as a critical reconstruction problem rather than simply assuming the canonical order reflects proclamation order.

## 1. God

The Qur'an presents God (`Allah`) as uniquely worthy of worship, creator and sovereign of the heavens and earth, knowing, living, sustaining, judging, merciful, powerful and incomparable.

Strong primary anchors include:

- Q 112:1-4: God is one, self-sufficient/dependable, neither begets nor is begotten, and has no comparable equal;
- Q 2:255: God is living/sustaining, owns heaven and earth, knows what is before/behind creatures, and is not wearied by preserving creation;
- Q 42:11: creator of heaven and earth, with nothing comparable to Him;
- Q 6:101: creator of all things; the text rejects divine offspring through its own argumentative logic.

Primary access:
- https://quran.com/al-ikhlas
- https://quran.com/2/255
- https://quran.com/42/11
- https://corpus.quran.com/translation.jsp?chapter=6&verse=101

### Semantic guard

The project will not flatten all later Islamic formulations into the Qur'an's wording.

For example:

- later technical `tawhid` theology can systematize Qur'anic monotheism;
- later debates about divine attributes, created/uncreated speech, anthropomorphism, and essence/attributes require later sources;
- Qur'anic statements about God's throne/seat, face, hand, nearness, knowledge and speech must first be registered proposition-by-proposition before later schools are used to interpret them.

## 2. Creation and cosmic sovereignty

Creation repeatedly functions as evidence for divine sovereignty, resurrection, providence and judgment.

The Qur'anic cosmos includes:

- heavens and earth;
- sun, moon, stars and natural signs;
- humans created by God;
- animals and plants;
- angels;
- jinn;
- Iblis/Satan;
- unseen realities and a final transformation/judgment of the present world.

Cosmology is not merely background physics. It is argumentative: creation is invoked to establish worship, gratitude, resurrection and divine power.

## 3. Adam, angels, Iblis and jinn

Q 2:30 introduces the placement of a human `khalifa`/successive authority on earth in dialogue with angels.

Q 18:50 explicitly identifies Iblis as `of the jinn` in the refusal-to-prostrate narrative.

Q 72 presents jinn as moral/religious hearers who can encounter Qur'anic proclamation and respond to it.

Primary access:
- https://quran.com/2/30
- https://corpus.quran.com/translation.jsp?chapter=18&verse=50
- https://quran.com/al-jinn

### Guard

`angel`, `jinn`, `shaytan`, and `Iblis` remain separate semantic identities unless a passage explicitly relates them. Later demonological systems must not be back-projected automatically.

## 4. Revelation and scripture

The Qur'an presents itself as revelation/recitation and locates that revelation within a larger history of divine communication.

Q 2:136 and 3:84 place Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, Moses, Jesus and other prophets in a broad revelation history.

Q 5:48 presents the Qur'anic `Book` as confirming prior scripture while also functioning as a criterion/guardian over it, and speaks of differentiated law/path among communities before final return to God.

Q 16:36 generalizes prophetic warning by saying that a messenger was sent among every community/nation with a call to worship God and avoid false objects of worship.

Q 17:88 makes a literary/rhetorical claim about the inimitability of the Qur'an against humans and jinn.

## 5. Prophetic history

The Qur'an repeatedly retells and reframes prophetic narratives rather than presenting one continuous historical chronicle.

Major figures include:

- Adam;
- Noah;
- Abraham;
- Lot;
- Ishmael and Isaac;
- Jacob/Joseph;
- Moses and Aaron;
- David and Solomon;
- Jonah;
- Zechariah and John;
- Mary and Jesus;
- other named messengers and unnamed messenger figures.

These narratives frequently function typologically/rhetorically:

1. messenger warns;
2. community disputes/rejects;
3. signs or argument are given;
4. judgment/vindication follows;
5. the pattern addresses the Qur'an's present audience.

This recurring structure must be distinguished from a modern claim that every prophetic narrative is intended primarily as linear biography.

## 6. Abraham and sacred history

Abraham is especially important as:

- monotheist exemplar;
- opponent of idolatry;
- ancestor within a wider prophetic lineage;
- figure linked to the sanctuary/House with Ishmael in Q 2;
- model of submission to God.

Later Islamic biography and ritual explanation may add narrative detail, but Qur'anic propositions should be registered first from the text itself.

## 7. Mary and Jesus

Mary and Jesus occupy a substantial but bounded place in the Qur'anic system.

The Qur'an presents Mary as specially chosen and gives extended annunciation/birth material. Jesus is Messiah, son of Mary, messenger, associated with signs and divine support, while the Qur'an rejects divine-sonship formulations and in Q 4:157-158 denies that Jesus' opponents killed/crucified him and says God raised him.

Primary anchors:
- Q 3:45-59;
- Q 4:157-171;
- Q 5:72-75, 110-120;
- Q 19:16-36.

Primary access example:
- https://quran.com/4/157

Method guard: this is one component of the Qur'anic system, not the project's organizing center. Later tafsir models of what precisely `shubbiha lahum` means are later interpretation and must be separated from the verse's wording.

## 8. Judgment, resurrection and afterlife

Eschatology is central rather than peripheral.

Q 75:3-4 argues explicitly that God can reassemble human bones. Other suras use cosmic-collapse imagery, resurrection, books/records, scales/accounting, witness, paradise/gardens and fire/punishment.

The Oxford Handbook survey of Qur'anic eschatology highlights:

- signs of the Hour/end;
- cessation/transformation of the present world;
- resurrection;
- judgment;
- reward/punishment;
- God's final eternal sovereignty.

Primary/scholarly access:
- https://quran.com/75/3-4
- https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34388/chapter-abstract/291614203

## 9. Community, ethics and ritual

The Qur'an addresses a developing believing community and contains norms concerning:

- prayer;
- almsgiving/charity;
- fasting;
- pilgrimage/sanctuary;
- food;
- marriage/divorce/inheritance;
- commercial ethics;
- warfare/peace/treaty;
- justice and testimony;
- care for poor/orphans/travelers;
- communal boundaries and relations with other groups.

Q 2:183 explicitly prescribes fasting while relating it to earlier communities.

Q 2:144's qibla instruction is primary evidence for prayer-direction boundary formation; later chronology reports about exactly when the change happened belong to later tradition unless independently corroborated.

Primary access:
- https://quran.com/2/183
- https://quran.com/2/144

## 10. The Qur'an's interlocutors

The Qur'an speaks about and to categories including:

- believers;
- disbelievers;
- associators/polytheists (`mushrikun`);
- People of the Book;
- Jews;
- Christians;
- hypocrites;
- earlier prophetic peoples;
- jinn.

These rhetorical categories must not be converted automatically into a demographic census of seventh-century Arabia.

A Qur'anic polemical category can be:

- a real social interlocutor;
- a generalized theological type;
- a scripturalized earlier people used to address the present;
- a mixture of those functions.

Historical identification requires evidence beyond label matching.

## 11. Chronology inside the corpus

Canonical sura order is not simply proclamation chronology.

Modern scholarship uses combinations of:

- style;
- rhyme/rhythm;
- sura structure;
- thematic development;
- address forms;
- legal/community complexity;
- intertextual relations;
- traditional Meccan/Medinan assignments.

Neuwirth broadly defends a refined version of the Nöldeke chronological project, while Sinai treats inner-Qur'anic chronology as a historical-critical enterprise. The project may use such models but must label them `SCHOLARLY_INTERPRETATION`, not Qur'anic self-dating.

## 12. Internal development without assuming contradiction or harmonization

Where passages differ in emphasis, V1 will preserve the difference before deciding among models such as:

- chronological development;
- audience/context shift;
- rhetorical variation;
- legal specification;
- redactional layering;
- semantic complementarity;
- unresolved tension.

The project will neither harmonize automatically nor manufacture contradiction for effect.

## V1 guards

Do not infer:

- later hadith/sira detail is already present in a sparse Qur'anic allusion;
- canonical order equals chronological order;
- `Meccan` and `Medinan` labels are primary-text dates;
- every Jewish/Christian parallel proves borrowing;
- every Arabic pagan term represents one uniform pre-Islamic religion;
- later Sunni or Shi'i doctrine is identical to the Qur'an's own conceptual vocabulary;
- Qur'anic Jesus material defines the entire Qur'anic religious system;
- a polemical category is automatically a precise historical community.
