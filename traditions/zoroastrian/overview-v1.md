# Zoroastrian / Iranian Mythos Overview V1

Status: `SOURCE-BASED OVERVIEW / REVIEW REQUIRED`

## Historical scope

Zoroastrian religious material is chronologically layered. V1 distinguishes at minimum:

- Old Avestan / Gathic material associated with Zarathustra;
- Younger Avestan material;
- Achaemenid-era Iranian religion and inscriptions;
- later Sasanian and Pahlavi systematization;
- still later Zoroastrian interpretation.

The fully articulated cosmological drama found in Middle Persian works such as the *Bundahishn* must not be silently projected backward into every Gathic verse.

## Ahura Mazda and cosmic order

The Gathic material presents **Ahura Mazda** as supreme lord associated with truth/order (`asha`) and creation. Human beings are morally responsible for choosing between truth/right order and falsehood/destructive orientation.

Yasna 30 is a central text for the `two spirits` and human choice. The exact theological relationship among Ahura Mazda, Spenta Mainyu, and Angra Mainyu has been interpreted differently across periods and scholarship.

V1 therefore avoids the simplistic formula `Zoroastrianism = two equal gods`.

## Dualism

Zoroastrian traditions develop a powerful opposition between beneficent and destructive principles.

In the Gathas, the two primal spirits are associated with opposed choices in thought, word, and deed. Later Pahlavi cosmology systematizes the opposition as Ohrmazd/Ahura Mazda versus Ahriman/Angra Mainyu in a finite cosmic conflict.

The later system is strongly dualistic but not an endless balance: evil is ultimately defeated.

## Creation, mixture, and final separation

Encyclopaedia Iranica's synthesis of later Zoroastrian cosmology describes a structured cosmic drama:

1. Ohrmazd's good creation;
2. the assault/mixing of evil with creation;
3. a bounded historical conflict;
4. eventual separation and elimination of evil.

The exact chronological scheme (including 9,000- or 12,000-year structures) belongs to later systematization and should not be attributed indiscriminately to the earliest texts.

The important mythic architecture is teleological: history has direction and evil is not permanent.

## Human moral agency

Choice is central in the Gathic material. Humans align themselves with truth/order or the Lie through thought, speech, and action.

This differs from mythologies in which humans primarily exist to relieve divine labor or sustain a city-god cult. Zoroastrian anthropology places unusually strong emphasis on moral participation in cosmic conflict.

## Death, judgment, and eschatology

Zoroastrian sources develop both individual and universal eschatology.

Shaul Shaked's Iranica synthesis stresses the need to separate:

- the individual's fate after death;
- universal final judgment;
- apocalyptic events leading toward the end;
- the final restoration/reconstitution of the world.

Later tradition includes developed concepts of postmortem judgment, differentiated destinations, final defeat of evil, resurrection/restoration, and a purified world.

V1 treats these as historically developing rather than assuming every component is equally explicit in the oldest strata.

## Yima and preservation traditions

Iranian tradition also preserves material around **Yima**, including a protected enclosure (`vara`) in which selected humans, animals, and plants are preserved from catastrophe.

This motif belongs in later comparison with flood/catastrophe-preservation stories, but resemblance alone does not establish direct borrowing.

## Comparison guard: Judaism, Christianity, and Islam

Zoroastrian eschatology has long been proposed as an influence on Jewish apocalypticism and, indirectly, Christianity and Islam. Iranica itself notes substantial scholarly arguments for influence on neighboring religions.

That is a legitimate transmission hypothesis, not an automatic fact.

For any specific proposed borrowing—resurrection, final judgment, angels/demons, cosmic dualism, savior figures, bridge judgment, apocalypse—the project must test:

- date of the Iranian evidence;
- date of the target Jewish/Christian/Islamic evidence;
- Persian-period or later contact pathways;
- whether the idea exists earlier in the target tradition;
- whether later Pahlavi evidence is being projected backward;
- whether common Indo-Iranian or broader Near Eastern backgrounds explain part of the resemblance.
