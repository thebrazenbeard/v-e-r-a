# Writing Sample — EU AI Act Enforcement Enters a New Phase

**Date:** 29 August 2026  
**Format:** Short policy brief  
**Topic:** European technology policy / AI governance

## Bottom line

The European Union’s AI Act moved from a largely preparatory regime into active enforcement in August 2026. The shift matters because the European Commission’s AI Office and national competent authorities now possess operative enforcement powers over several major parts of the framework, while important high-risk-system provisions remain on a later timetable. For companies operating across jurisdictions, the practical consequence is not simply “the AI Act is now in force”; it is that compliance risk is becoming more differentiated by system type, provider role, deployment context, and date of market entry.

## What changed in August 2026

The Commission states that from 2 August 2026 the AI Office and Member State authorities began enforcing applicable AI Act provisions. The AI Office has direct responsibility for general-purpose AI model providers, including systemic-risk models, while national authorities carry much of the enforcement burden for other regulated AI systems.

General-purpose AI obligations themselves began applying on 2 August 2025. Those obligations include technical documentation, information for downstream providers, copyright-compliance policies, and published summaries of training content. For general-purpose models presenting systemic risk, the regime adds evaluation, risk-mitigation, incident-reporting, and cybersecurity duties. The significance of August 2026 is that the Commission can now enforce those obligations, including through financial penalties.

A separate transparency layer also became applicable on 2 August 2026. Certain interactive AI systems must make clear that users are interacting with AI rather than a human. The framework also imposes disclosure or marking duties for categories of synthetic or manipulated content, including deepfakes. The exact obligation depends on whether the regulated party is a provider or deployer and on the type of content or system involved.

## Strategic implications

First, firms should distinguish between **application dates** and **enforcement dates**. Some obligations were legally applicable before August 2026 even though the enforcement architecture was still maturing. Conversely, several high-risk-system requirements remain on later schedules. Treating the Act as a single switch flipped in August 2026 will produce both over-compliance and dangerous gaps.

Second, companies outside the EU cannot assume geography removes them from the compliance problem. Providers placing covered general-purpose models on the EU market may face EU obligations, and the Commission’s guidance includes requirements concerning authorised representatives for certain non-EU providers. Global model providers therefore have an incentive to design documentation, copyright, safety, and transparency controls that can withstand EU scrutiny rather than treating Europe as an isolated compliance appendix.

Third, downstream businesses need better supplier diligence. The AI Act creates documentation flows from general-purpose model providers to downstream AI-system providers. A company integrating third-party models should know what evidence it can obtain about model capabilities, limitations, training-content disclosure, and compliance posture. The regulatory burden is therefore likely to propagate through procurement and vendor-management processes even where the downstream firm is not itself the original model provider.

Finally, the enforcement regime remains staggered. The Commission’s August 2026 enforcement guidance notes that some high-risk AI-system provisions will apply later, including rules scheduled for 2027 and 2028. The policy challenge for businesses is therefore continuous: compliance programs must track which duties attach now, which attach later, and which depend on future technical standards or implementation guidance.

## Assessment

The EU has entered the period in which AI governance becomes an operational compliance issue rather than primarily a legislative one. The near-term winners will not necessarily be firms with the largest policy teams. They will be firms that can map their exact role in the AI value chain, maintain sourceable technical and governance documentation, and update controls as the staged timetable advances.

For policymakers, the central test is enforcement consistency. A regime split among the AI Office, national authorities, and sector-specific regulators can create uneven interpretations if coordination is weak. The next year will therefore reveal whether the EU can convert a detailed legal architecture into predictable supervisory practice without freezing legitimate experimentation.
